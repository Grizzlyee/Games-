## 1. IRT basics (Rasch/1PL, 2PL, 3PL): what "difficulty" means, why CAT targets ~50%, and how practice targets differ

### Takeaway
In IRT, an item's difficulty *b* is the ability level at which a learner has a 50% chance of success (1PL/2PL), so "difficulty" and "ability" live on the same scale; CAT for *measurement* picks items near P≈0.5 because that is where a Rasch item gives the most information, but systems built for *practice/learning* deliberately aim higher (≈60–90% correct, most often ≈75%) to trade a little measurement precision for motivation and learning.

### Cited Findings
- Under the Rasch model the item information function peaks where examinee ability equals item difficulty, i.e., where P(correct)=0.5; the maximum information value is 0.25 because information = P(1−P). **[SECONDARY, psychometric software blog]** — [dexter: "The information game" (2020)](https://dexter-psychometrics.github.io/dexter/articles/blog/2020-05-27-the-information-game); see also [Computerized adaptive testing: implementation issues (arXiv 1012.0042)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.0042)
- Picking the maximum-information item under Rasch is therefore equivalent to picking the item a person at the current ability estimate has a 50% chance of answering correctly; Chang & Ying showed maximum-information selection under Rasch gives a consistent, asymptotically normal ability estimator, but the approach needs modification for 2PL/3PL (otherwise the estimator may be inconsistent). **[PR]** — [Chang & Ying (2009), "Nonlinear sequential designs for logistic IRT models with applications to computerized adaptive tests," *Annals of Statistics* 37(3)](https://projecteuclid.org/download/pdfview_1/euclid.aos/1239369028)
- Elo-based adaptive *practice* systems deliberately sample items with a mean success probability of 0.75, "making the tasks challenging yet not too difficult" (Math Garden). **[PR]** — [Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas (2011), *Computers & Education* 57(2), 1813–1824, doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2011.02.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2011.02.003) (abstract via [ERIC EJ925823](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823))
- A Math Garden presentation by van der Maas describes the design goal as keeping success probability at a constant high level of .6, .75 or .9, and notes that children prefer P+ > .75. **[SECONDARY — slide deck, content seen only via search excerpt]** — [van der Maas, "Educational data mining: Five years of Math Garden," Leiden expert meeting (2014)](https://web.universiteitleiden.nl/fsw/mtlab/ExpM2014/Expert%20meeting%202014%20-%20Han%20van%20der%20Maas%20-%20Educational%20data%20mining.pdf)
- Standard IRT model forms (for reference; standard textbook definitions, consistent with the sources above): 1PL/Rasch P(θ)=1/(1+e^−(θ−b)); 2PL adds discrimination *a*: 1/(1+e^−a(θ−b)); 3PL adds a lower asymptote ("guessing") *c*: c+(1−c)/(1+e^−a(θ−b)). Discrimination and guessing are the reasons the Rasch "50% = most informative" result does not carry over unchanged to 2PL/3PL — [Chang & Ying (2009)](https://projecteuclid.org/download/pdfview_1/euclid.aos/1239369028)

### Inferences
- **Difficulty is relative, not absolute.** An item rated "difficulty 3" is only meaningful relative to a learner's ability on the same scale. For a weekly practice exam, the useful quantity is the *gap* (learner ability − item difficulty), which maps to an expected success probability.
- **Guessing raises the "most-informative" success rate above 50% for multiple choice.** Using Lord's standard 3PL result (θ at max information = b + (1/a)·ln[(1+√(1+8c))/2]; textbook result, not re-verified in this session), I computed that for a 5-option item (c≈0.2) the most informative item is one the learner gets right ≈65% of the time; for 4 options (c≈0.25) ≈68%. For short-answer items (c=0, e.g., CSAT math 단답형) it stays at 50%.
- **Measurement vs. learning tension.** Items near 50% (60–65% for 5-option MC) give the best ability *estimates*; items near 75–85% are what practice systems use for *learning/motivation*. A weekly practice exam can serve both by putting most items in the practice band and a minority (≈20%) near the measurement point.
- Logistic reference table (my computation; 1 "level" = 1 logit; MC column assumes 5-option guessing floor 0.2):

| gap = ability − item level | P(correct), short-answer | P(correct), 5-option MC |
|---|---|---|
| −2 | 12% | 30% |
| −1 | 27% | 42% |
| 0 | 50% | 60% |
| +0.5 | 62% | 70% |
| +1 | 73% | 79% |
| +1.5 | 82% | 85% |
| +2 | 88% | 91% |
| +3 | 95% | 96% |

## 2. Elo-based learner modeling for adaptive practice (Math Garden; Pelánek; target difficulty experiments)

### Takeaway
Elo treats each answer as a "match" between learner and item, updating both ratings by K×(actual − expected); Math Garden (Rekentuin) samples items at ≈75% expected success, and the experiments on target difficulty conflict: in children's math, higher success targets increased the amount practiced and performance gains (Jansen et al. 2013), whereas in an online geography system harder questions (higher error targets) were better for learning and long-term engagement while easier ones were better for short-term engagement (Papoušek, Stanislav & Pelánek 2016).

### Cited Findings
- **Math Garden (Klinkenberg et al. 2011)**: a computerized adaptive *practice* (CAP) system for primary-school arithmetic; it applies a new estimation method based on Elo's (1978) chess rating system to estimate ability and item difficulty "on the fly"; items are sampled with a mean success probability of 0.75. **[PR]** — [Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas (2011), *Computers & Education* 57(2), 1813–1824](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2011.02.003); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ925823)
- Math Garden scale: as of 2018, more than 452,000 K-12 children and 5,000 Dutch primary schools used it. **[PR, conference]** — ["Governing games: Adaptive game selection in the Math Garden," ITM Web of Conferences (ICTeSSH 2020)](https://www.itm-conferences.org/articles/itmconf/pdf/2020/03/itmconf_ictessh2020_03003.pdf)
- A decade-long methodology overview of Math Garden (model fit, real-time analytics challenges): **[PR]** — [Brinkhuis, Savi, Hofman, Coomans, van der Maas & Maris (2018), "Learning as it happens: A decade of analyzing and shaping a large-scale online learning system," *Journal of Learning Analytics* 5(2), 29–46](https://learning-analytics.info/index.php/JLA/article/view/5796)
- **Pelánek (2016)**: interprets a student's answer as a match between student and item so the system "can easily dynamically estimate the skill of students and difficulty of items"; gives a systematic overview of Elo variants in education, compares Elo with alternative methods, includes a case study of adaptive geography practice, and argues Elo is "simple, robust, and effective" with specific guidelines for use. **[PR]** — [Pelánek (2016), "Applications of the Elo rating system in adaptive educational systems," *Computers & Education* 98, 169–179, doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2016.03.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.03.017)
- Basic educational Elo (standard formulation as described in Pelánek 2016 — formula not re-read from full text this session): P(correct) = 1/(1+e^−(θ_student − d_item)); after the answer, θ ← θ + K·(correct − P) and d ← d + K·(P − correct), where correct ∈ {0,1}. — [Pelánek (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.03.017)
- Papoušek & Pelánek's Elo variant replaces a constant K with an "uncertainty function" (K decreasing with the number of answers seen), giving "fast coarse estimates after a few answers and stability in the long run." **[PR]** — [Papoušek, Pelánek & Stanislav (2014), "Adaptive Practice of Facts in Domains with Varied Prior Knowledge," EDM 2014](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/EDM14-adaptive-facts.pdf); extended in [Pelánek, Papoušek, Řihák, Stanislav & Nižnan (2017), "Elo-based learner modeling for the adaptive practice of facts," *User Modeling and User-Adapted Interaction*, doi:10.1007/s11257-016-9185-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11257-016-9185-7)
- A 2025 UMUAI paper investigates a *dynamic K* for Elo in adaptive learning, adapting K by the number of observations and time between sessions (its trend-adaptation α optimum ≈ .1 — not the same α as the uncertainty function). **[PR]** — ["Balancing stability and flexibility: investigating a dynamic K value approach for the Elo rating system in adaptive learning environments," *UMUAI* (2025), doi:10.1007/s11257-025-09439-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11257-025-09439-z)
- Related Math Garden work on rating uncertainty: **[PR]** — ["Tracking with (Un)Certainty," *Journal of Intelligence* 8(1):10 (2020), doi:10.3390/jintelligence8010010](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence8010010)
- **Jansen et al. (2013)**: 207 children, grades 3–6; a control group plus three experimental conditions that used the computer-adaptive math program for six weeks and differed only in the **pre-set success rate** (difficulty adapted to each child's ability so success rates were comparable across ability levels). Math anxiety decreased about equally in all conditions; perceived competence improved only modestly; math performance improved only in the experimental conditions; **higher pre-set success rates led to more problems attempted and larger performance gains.** **[PR]** — [Jansen, Louwerse, Straatemeier, Van der Ven, Klinkenberg & Van der Maas (2013), *Learning and Individual Differences* 24, 190–197](https://dare.uva.nl/id/789fc422-4935-49ca-bcba-803239522951); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1041608012001951)
- A later Math Garden study found an increase in math self-concept after ~20 weeks of practice but no effect on math anxiety; it describes Math Garden as adapting difficulty so students average about 75% correct. **[PR]** — ["How to Continue? New Approaches to Investigating the Effects of Adaptive Math Learning Programs on Students' Performance, Self-Concept, and Anxiety," *Journal of Intelligence* 11(6):108 (2023)](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-3200/11/6/108) ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10299571/))
- **Papoušek, Stanislav & Pelánek (2016)**: randomized trial in an open online geography practice system with four conditions differing only in **target error rate: 5%, 20%, 35%, 50%** (i.e., 95/80/65/50% success); the system adapts question construction so achieved performance is close to the target. Easier questions helped **short-term** engagement; harder questions helped **long-term engagement and learning** — "a conflict between learning and long term engagement on one side (more difficult is better), and short term engagement on the other." The authors stress careful formalization of optimization goals and flag attrition bias. **[PR]** — [Papoušek, Stanislav & Pelánek (2016), "Impact of Question Difficulty on Engagement and Learning," ITS 2016, LNCS, doi:10.1007/978-3-319-39583-8_28](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-39583-8_28); [tech report FIMU-RS-2016-02](https://www.fi.muni.cz/reports/files/2016/FIMU-RS-2016-02.pdf); [publication record](https://is.muni.cz/publication/1350112/en/Impact-of-Question-Difficulty-on-Engagement-and-Learning/Papousek-Stanislav-Pelanek?lang=en)
- **Theoretical "85% rule"**: for gradient-descent learning on binary classification with Gaussian noise, the optimal training error rate is ≈15.87% (≈85% accuracy), which can give exponential speed-ups in learning; tested on artificial neural networks and a computational-neuroscience model of perceptual learning — not in classrooms. Press coverage notes it applied only to simple tasks with clear right/wrong answers. **[PR]** — [Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019), "The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning," *Nature Communications* 10, 4646, doi:10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579); [press summary (Neuroscience News)](https://neurosciencenews.com/learning-optimized-15171/)

### Inferences
- The evidence brackets a practice target of roughly **65–85% correct**: ≈75% is the most-used engineering default (Math Garden); a 15% error rate is a theoretical optimum for a narrow class of learners; very easy practice (≥90–95%) keeps people engaged short-term but, in the geography trial, produced less learning; very hard practice (50%) helped learning there but is the CAT measurement point and risks discouragement.
- For a **motivated adult** facing a high-stakes exam (unlike young children in Jansen et al.), the Papoušek et al. result argues for the harder half of the band (≈70–75%), while Jansen et al. warn that if the learner starts doing fewer problems (dropping engagement), easing difficulty raises practice volume. A pragmatic rule: target **≈75% overall, allowed band 65–85%**, and move toward 70% while engagement is good, toward 80–85% if the learner skips sessions or reports discouragement.
- Elo is attractive for hand use because each update is one subtraction and one multiplication, and it estimates learner and item on the same scale. But Elo's *item* updates rely on many learners answering the same item; with **one learner**, item difficulty cannot be separated from the learner's ability (see Q5/Q6).

## 3. Knowledge tracing (BKT, PFA) and mastery thresholds in practice (ASSISTments, Khan Academy, MATHia, ALEKS)

### Takeaway
Production systems decide "mastered" with simple, transparent rules (ASSISTments: 3 correct in a row; Khan Academy: 70–85% for Familiar, 100% for Proficient/Mastered; BKT tutors: P(known) ≥ ~0.95) or, in ALEKS, by mapping which topics a learner is "ready to learn"; these translate into weekly-exam rules like "N consecutive/most-recent correct at a level before moving up."

### Cited Findings
- **BKT**: attributed to Corbett & Anderson (1995); a two-state (known/unknown) hidden Markov model with four per-skill parameters — prior mastery P(L0), learning/transit P(T), guess P(G), slip P(S); used notably in the Cognitive Tutor family of curricula (Carnegie Learning, predecessor of MATHia). **[PR]** — [Baker et al., Behaviormetrika paper on knowledge tracing](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/behaviormetrika_vfinal.pdf); original: [Corbett & Anderson (1995), "Knowledge tracing: Modeling the acquisition of procedural knowledge," *UMUAI* 4, 253–278, doi:10.1007/BF01099821](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01099821)
- A **P(mastery) ≥ 0.95** cutoff is described as a common operational threshold, after which the tutor stops drilling the skill; practitioner heuristics suggest keeping guess < ~0.3 and slip < ~0.1 to avoid degenerate fits. **[SECONDARY]** — [mindomax, "The math behind knowledge tracing"](https://www.mindomax.com/the-math-behind-knowledge-tracing); [metricgate docs](https://metricgate.com/docs/sequential-irt-knowledge-tracing/)
- **PFA**: logistic regression predicting correctness from a skill (KC) difficulty term β plus weighted counts of the student's prior **successes** (weight γ) and prior **failures** (weight ρ) on that skill: logit P = β + γ·s + ρ·f (summed over the item's skills); extends the Additive Factors Model by splitting attempts into successes/failures and typically outperforms AFM; parameters fit by maximum likelihood. A negative ρ does not mean "negative learning" — failures carry more evidence of not knowing than the practice gain. **[PR, original + secondary descriptions]** — Pavlik, Cen & Koedinger (2009), "Performance Factors Analysis – A New Alternative to Knowledge Tracing," AIED 2009, as described in [arXiv 1501.02732](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.02732), [PFA tutorial module](https://yilinl.quarto.pub/pfa/), [Penn MOOT slides](https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/MOOT/slides/W004V003.pdf), and [JEDM article](https://jedm.educationaldatamining.org/index.php/JEDM/article/download/402/107)
- **ASSISTments Skill Builders**: a student must answer **three in a row correct** to demonstrate mastery; any incorrect answer or any hint resets the streak to zero; if mastery isn't reached within **10 problems**, the student cannot continue until the next calendar day (teachers can reset); pools contain 20–100 problems per skill; logged statuses include "limit exceeded" and "not mastered exhausted." **[VENDOR — nonprofit platform documentation]** — [ASSISTments: Skill Builders](https://www.assistments.org/individual-resource/skillbuilders); [What are Skill Builders…](https://www.assistments.org/individual-resource/what-are-skill-builders-and-how-are-they-different-from-the-other-content-within-assistments); [ASSISTments data dump glossary](https://sites.google.com/site/assistmentstestbed/4-analyze-data/ali-doc-request/data-dump-glossary)
- **Khan Academy mastery levels**: each skill is worth 100 Mastery Points (Familiar = 50, Proficient = 80, Mastered = 100). Not started/Attempted → Familiar: typically by scoring **70–85%** on an exercise (or 100% on a quiz/test/challenge). Familiar → Proficient: all questions on the skill right in an exercise, quiz, test or challenge. Proficient → Mastered: all right on a Unit Test or Course Challenge. A 100% first attempt can jump two levels to Proficient; Mastered can drop two levels to Familiar if scoring < 70% on an exercise; wrong answers in practice do not lower levels, but wrong answers in quizzes/unit tests can. Course Mastery counts only Proficient and Mastered skills. **[VENDOR]** — [Khan Academy Help Center, "How do Khan Academy's Mastery levels work?"](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/5548760867853); [Course and Unit Mastery](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/115002552631)
- **ALEKS / Knowledge Space Theory** (Doignon & Falmagne, c. 1985): a learner's *knowledge state* is the subset of problems they can solve; ALEKS infers the feasible state rather than a single score. The **outer fringe** = items not yet in the state that could be added (interpreted as "ready to learn"); the **inner fringe** = most recently acquired items; in a learning space a state is fully determined by its inner and outer fringes. **[PR/academic + VENDOR]** — [Wikipedia: Knowledge space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_space) (citing Doignon & Falmagne 1999; Falmagne & Doignon 2011); [McGraw-Hill, "ALEKS: Assessing a learning space"](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-assessing-learning-space.pdf); [IJAIED 2019, doi:10.1007/s40593-019-00176-0](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40593-019-00176-0)
- One analysis of 1,940,473 elementary math learning occasions reported a median ≈0.93 probability that a student successfully learned an outer-fringe ("ready to learn") item. **[SECONDARY summary of ALEKS research — original not opened]** — [McGraw-Hill ALEKS research PDF](https://www.mheducation.com/content/dam/mhe/research/prek-12/essa-tier-4/aleks-assessing-learning-space.pdf) / [emergentmind ALEKS topic page](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/aleks)

### Inferences
- Common pattern across systems: (1) mastery is decided on **recent, consecutive or high-percentage** performance, not lifetime averages; (2) **practice** mistakes are forgiven, **test** mistakes count (Khan); (3) there is a **stopping/limit rule** for unproductive practice (ASSISTments' 10-a-day); (4) the next item comes from the "ready to learn" frontier (ALEKS) — i.e., skills whose prerequisites are mastered.
- For a weekly 20–30-item exam, BKT/PFA are too heavy to fit by hand, but PFA's idea (separate weights for recent successes vs failures; failures weigh more) can be approximated: count a recent wrong answer as stronger evidence than a recent right answer.
- A hand-applicable mastery rule mixing these: a skill at level L is "mastered" when the learner gets ≥ 80% over the last ≥ 8–10 attempts at level ≥ L spread over ≥ 2 weeks, **and** the most recent 3 attempts at that level are correct (ASSISTments-style streak); demote if a later exam gives < 60–70% on that skill (Khan-style).

### Gaps
- No primary Carnegie Learning/MATHia documentation was accessed to confirm the 0.95 threshold or current MATHia mastery logic; 0.95 is widely attributed to Corbett & Anderson's tutors but only secondary sources were seen this session.
- ALEKS's exact assessment-item-selection algorithm and its "ready to learn" display rules were not obtained from primary documentation.
- Whether the Khan Academy rules differ between classic and newer product versions was not resolved.

## 4. Duolingo/SRS-style models (half-life regression, FSRS) — relevance to item selection

### Takeaway
SRS models schedule a review when predicted recall drops to a target (FSRS default 90%; HLR models recall p = 2^(−Δ/h)); for weekly exams this translates into a review quota: re-test previously learned/missed items on expanding intervals so that they are still ~85–90% likely to be recalled.

### Cited Findings
- **Half-life regression (Duolingo)**: models recall probability p = 2^(−Δ/h), where Δ is time since the item was last practiced (days) and h is the learner's memory half-life for that item; h is estimated as h = 2^(Θ·x) from features x (e.g., counts of prior correct/incorrect answers). **[PR — ACL; authors are Duolingo employees]** — [Settles & Meeder (2016), "A Trainable Spaced Repetition Model for Language Learning," ACL 2016, 1848–1858, doi:10.18653/v1/P16-1174](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174); [Duolingo blog, "How we learn how you learn"](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn) **[VENDOR]**
- Duolingo reports HLR's error rate as nearly half that of the Leitner system used in its first version; code (MIT) and 13 million learning traces are public. **[VENDOR claim]** — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn); [github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression](https://github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression)
- **FSRS**: schedules each review for when predicted retrievability R falls to the user's **desired retention (default 0.9)**; "stability" S is defined as the interval at which R = 0.9, so at the default the next interval ≈ S; FSRS-4.5 uses a power forgetting curve with decay −0.5 and factor 19/81. **[COMMUNITY]** — [Borretti, "Implementing FSRS in 100 Lines"](https://borretti.me/article/implementing-fsrs-in-100-lines); [ankiR `fsrs_interval` docs](https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/ankiR/versions/0.6.0/topics/fsrs_interval); [AnkiWeb forum: "How do I use FSRS for exam?"](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/how-do-i-use-fsrs-for-exam/43631)

### Inferences
- For CSAT prep, SRS logic matters most for memorization-heavy content (English vocabulary, 한국사 facts, 통합사회/통합과학 concepts) and for **re-testing previously missed items (오답노트)**. A Leitner-style weekly rule is the hand-runnable proxy: an item answered wrong returns next week; each subsequent correct answer doubles its interval (1 → 2 → 4 → 8 weeks); a miss resets it to 1 week.
- The SRS target (~90% recall) is *higher* than the new-learning target (~75%): review items should be easy-ish by design; they consolidate rather than stretch.

### Gaps
- The official FSRS wiki (github.com/open-spaced-repetition) was not accessed; constants above come from community implementations. The peer-reviewed FSRS-predecessor paper (Ye et al., KDD 2022) was not checked this session.
- No evidence found on SRS scheduling for complex problem-solving items (e.g., CSAT math 킬러 문항), as opposed to facts/vocabulary.

## 5. Simple, transparent heuristics: staircases (Levitt 1971), Elo update with K, initializing difficulty without data, and updating from observed correct rates

### Takeaway
Two hand-runnable controllers converge to a chosen success rate: Levitt's transformed up-down staircases (2-down/1-up → 70.7%, 3-down/1-up → 79.4%) and a fixed-target Elo-style update; item difficulty can be seeded by expert rating or by published population correct rates, but with a single learner it can only be recalibrated via anchor items, not estimated per item.

### Cited Findings
- **Levitt (1971)**: transformed up-down methods; the rule's convergence point is the stimulus level where the probability of the "down" sequence equals 0.5. Tabulated convergence points include **0.707** (2-down/1-up) and **0.794** (3-down/1-up), the two most commonly used variants; the paper has ~4,000 Scopus citations. **[PR]** — [Levitt (1971), "Transformed Up-Down Methods in Psychoacoustics," *J. Acoust. Soc. Am.* 49(2B), 467–477, doi:10.1121/1.1912375](https://doi.org/10.1121/1.1912375); table reproduced in [*Behavior Research Methods* (2024), doi:10.3758/s13428-024-02430-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-024-02430-3)
- Elo update (educational form): θ ← θ + K(correct − P), d ← d + K(P − correct); K constant or decreasing with number of answers (uncertainty function) to get fast early estimates and later stability. **[PR]** — [Pelánek (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.03.017); [Papoušek et al. (2014)](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/EDM14-adaptive-facts.pdf); [dynamic-K study, UMUAI 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11257-025-09439-z)
- The "cold start" (no data for new items/learners) is a recognized issue addressed with Elo warm-up approaches. **[PR, title-level only]** — ["A warm-up for adaptive online learning environments – the Elo rating approach for assessing the cold start problem" (ResearchGate record)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346425657_A_warm-up_for_adaptive_online_learning_environments_-_the_Elo_rating_approach_for_assessing_the_cold_start_problem)

### Inferences
- **Staircase math (verifiable arithmetic):** an N-down/1-up rule converges where P^N = 0.5 → P = 0.5^(1/N): N=1 → 50%, N=2 → 70.7%, N=3 → 79.4%, N=4 → 84.1%. At the weekly-exam level, a "2-down/1-up" analog would be: raise difficulty after two consecutive weeks at/above target, lower after one week below.
- **Weighted up-down = fixed-target Elo.** Stepping harder by s after each correct and easier by s·p/(1−p) after each wrong converges to success rate p (equilibrium: p·s = (1−p)·s·p/(1−p)). For p = 0.75 the "easier" step is 3× the "harder" step. This is exactly an Elo update with P fixed at 0.75: Δ = K(1−0.75)=+0.25K after a correct, Δ = K(0−0.75)=−0.75K after a wrong. (Commonly attributed to Kaernbach 1991, *Perception & Psychophysics* — not verified this session.)
- **Item difficulty without data** (two sources of priors):
  1. *Expert rating* on a 1–5 scale with explicit anchors (e.g., 1 = one-step recall of a core concept; 3 = standard multi-step 기출 type; 5 = 킬러/준킬러 multi-concept synthesis).
  2. *Population correct rate* for real past items (기출/모의평가 정답률 as published by exam-analysis services), converted with a band table, e.g., ≥85% → L1, 70–85% → L2, 50–70% → L3, 30–50% → L4, < 30% → L5. With 5-option MC this makes "level 3 ≈ an average examinee gets it right ~60%," i.e., ≈1 logit per level, consistent with the table in Q1.
- **Updating item difficulty with one learner:** Elo's item update needs many independent learners; one learner's results can't distinguish "item easier than rated" from "learner stronger than estimated." Workable substitutes: (a) keep **anchor items** (real past items with population correct rates) fixed and let only ability move; (b) use the learner's results to recalibrate the *tutor's rating scale as a whole* (if tutor-made "L4" items are consistently answered like anchor "L3" items over ≥ 3 weeks, shift all future tutor-made L4 ratings down ~0.5); (c) re-attempts of the same item are contaminated by memory and should not be used to re-rate it.

### Gaps
- No source accessed that validates the specific 1–5 band cut-points; they are a proposal.
- Availability and reliability of item-level 정답률 for Korean CSAT/모의평가 items was not researched here (check sibling notes / EBSi).
- Kaernbach (1991) weighted up-down not verified.

## 6. Recommended manual procedure: (a) tag items 1–5, (b) per-skill accuracy, (c) next exam's difficulty mix for a target band, (d) small samples (~20 items/week)

### Takeaway
Use a 1–5 level scale where 1 level ≈ 1 logit, keep one ability number per subject-skill on the same scale, predict each item's success from the ability–level gap via a lookup table, update ability weekly by K×(observed − expected)/n with shrinkage and caps, and assemble each exam as ~60% core (≈75–80% predicted), ~20% stretch (≈50–60%), ~20% spaced review (≈90%) so the whole exam is predicted at ≈75% (band 65–85%).

### Cited Findings
- Practice systems target ≈75% (Math Garden) — [Klinkenberg et al. (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2011.02.003); theoretical optimum ≈85% for simple binary learners — [Wilson et al. (2019)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579); harder (50–65% success) was better for learning and long-term engagement in an online system, easier (≥ 80–95%) for short-term engagement — [Papoušek, Stanislav & Pelánek (2016)](https://rd.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-39583-8_28); higher success rates increased practice volume and gains in children — [Jansen et al. (2013)](https://dare.uva.nl/id/789fc422-4935-49ca-bcba-803239522951).
- Items near P=0.5 maximize measurement information (Rasch) — [dexter blog (2020)](https://dexter-psychometrics.github.io/dexter/articles/blog/2020-05-27-the-information-game); [Chang & Ying (2009)](https://projecteuclid.org/download/pdfview_1/euclid.aos/1239369028).
- Streak/percentage mastery rules used in production — [ASSISTments](https://www.assistments.org/individual-resource/skillbuilders) **[VENDOR]**; [Khan Academy](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/5548760867853) **[VENDOR]**.
- Decreasing K (uncertainty function) for early speed and later stability — [Papoušek et al. (2014)](https://www.fi.muni.cz/~xpelanek/publications/EDM14-adaptive-facts.pdf); [Pelánek (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2016.03.017).
- Review scheduling at a recall target (FSRS default 0.9) — [Borretti (FSRS explainer)](https://borretti.me/article/implementing-fsrs-in-100-lines) **[COMMUNITY]**; HLR p = 2^(−Δ/h) — [Settles & Meeder (2016)](https://aclanthology.org/P16-1174).

### Inferences
*(Everything below is a synthesized proposal built on the findings above; numbers are design choices, not empirically validated for CSAT.)*

**(a) Tag each item (columns in the item log)**
- `item_id | subject | skill (단원/유형) | format (MC5 / short-answer) | level 1–5 | level_source (expert / 정답률 / anchor) | date_used`.
- Level definition: 1 level ≈ 1 logit. Expert anchors per subject (e.g., L1 one-step concept recall; L2 routine single-skill; L3 standard multi-step 기출 type; L4 준킬러 two-concept or trap-heavy; L5 킬러 multi-concept synthesis). If a population correct rate exists for 5-option MC: ≥85% → L1, 70–85% → L2, 50–70% → L3, 30–50% → L4, <30% → L5. Half-levels (2.5, 3.5) allowed.
- Mark ≥ 3–5 items per subject each week as **anchors** (real past items with known 정답률) when available.

**(b) Per-skill ability and accuracy (weekly sheet)**
- Keep one ability value θ per subject-skill on the 1–5 scale (start: from a diagnostic exam, set θ to the level where the learner scores ~60% on MC / ~50% on short-answer; if no diagnostic, θ = 3).
- For each answered item: gap = θ − level → expected P from the lookup (MC5: −1→42%, 0→60%, +0.5→70%, +1→79%, +1.5→85%, +2→91%; short-answer: −1→27%, 0→50%, +1→73%, +2→88%). Score x = 1 correct, 0 wrong; optionally x = 0.5 if the learner marked it "guessed" or exceeded the time budget.
- Per skill this week: n, O = Σx, E = ΣP.
- **Update:** θ_new = θ + K·(O − E)/n, with K = 2.0 while the skill has < 15 total attempts, then K = 1.0; cap |Δθ| ≤ 1.0 per week. (Example: 5 items, E = 3.75, O = 5 → Δθ = 2·1.25/5 = +0.5; O = 2 → Δθ = −0.7.) This is a damped version of the Rasch maximum-likelihood step (full step = (O − E)/ΣP(1−P) ≈ 5.3·(O−E)/n at P≈.75), shrunk because weekly samples are small — consistent with the "decreasing K" idea. Per-item sequential Elo (θ ← θ + 0.4(x − P), 0.2 after 15 attempts) gives nearly the same result.
- Also report smoothed accuracy for humans: acc_smoothed = (O + 2·E/n)/(n + 2) (adds two pseudo-items at the predicted rate) and a 4-week rolling accuracy.

**(c) Build next week's exam to hit the target band**
- Overall target 75% predicted correct (allowed 65–85%). Mix for a 20–30-item exam:
  - **Core ~60%**: level ≈ θ − 1 (MC5 ≈ 79%, short-answer ≈ 73%).
  - **Stretch ~20%**: level ≈ θ (MC5 ≈ 60%, short-answer ≈ 50%) — also the most informative for estimating θ.
  - **Review ~20%**: previously missed items / earlier skills on a Leitner schedule (1 → 2 → 4 → 8 weeks; reset to 1 on a miss), at level ≤ θ − 2 (≈90%).
  - Predicted total ≈ 0.6·0.79 + 0.2·0.60 + 0.2·0.91 ≈ 78% for MC; check ΣP/N before finalizing and swap items until it lands in 72–80%.
- Skill allocation: prioritize skills that are (i) high exam weight, (ii) not yet mastered, (iii) whose prerequisites are mastered (ALEKS "ready to learn"); ≥ 3 items per actively-measured skill, 3–5 focus skills per week.
- **Exam-level dial (staircase on top):** if last exam's actual accuracy > 85% → shift all target levels +0.5; if < 65% → shift −0.5; otherwise keep. Optionally require two consecutive weeks > 85% before raising (2-down/1-up spirit), but lower after one bad week.
- **Mastery:** skill mastered at level L when ≥ 80% over last ≥ 8–10 attempts at level ≥ L across ≥ 2 weeks and the last 3 at that level are correct → raise its target level and move it to review rotation; demote if a later exam shows < 60–70%.
- Monthly: one full, timed, real-format mock (measurement exam), not adapted, to check CSAT-relevant performance; don't mix its score into the practice targets.

**(d) Small-sample safeguards**
- Binomial noise (my arithmetic, p≈0.75): SE of a proportion is ±19 pp (95% CI) for n = 20, ±15 pp for n = 30, ±27 pp for n = 10, ±38 pp for n = 5. So: use the **whole-exam** accuracy (n = 20–30) for the global difficulty dial; treat **per-skill** numbers (n ≈ 3–6) only as directional until ≥ 10 cumulative attempts.
- Shrinkage (small K, pseudo-counts), per-week caps (|Δθ| ≤ 1), a dead band (65–85% → no change), rolling 3–4-week windows, and no mastery decision on one week's data.
- Distinguish "slip" from "gap": a single wrong answer on a mastered skill (BKT "slip") shouldn't demote; two misses at the same level within 2 weeks should.
- With one learner, never re-rate individual items from that learner's results; recalibrate the tutor's rating scale via anchors (Q5).
- Record why items were missed (concept / careless / time / misread) — careless/time misses can be scored 0.5 for θ updates but still enter the review queue.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests weekly-batch adaptive practice exams for adult high-stakes exam preparation; all targets are extrapolated from children's math (Jansen; Math Garden), online geography practice (Papoušek et al.) and theory (Wilson et al.).
- The K values, band cut-points, 60/20/20 mix and Leitner intervals are design choices that should be revisited after ~6–8 weeks of logged data (e.g., check that predicted ΣP matched actual scores within ±10 pp on average).
- CSAT-specific facts (item counts, MC vs short-answer share by subject, 2028 reform format, availability of item-level 정답률) were not researched in this note and need confirmation from sibling notes or official KICE/EBSi sources.
