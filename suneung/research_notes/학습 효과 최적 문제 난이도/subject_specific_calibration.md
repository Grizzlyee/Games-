## Q1. Foreign-language (English) reading: lexical coverage thresholds, vocabulary size, and extensive reading / "i+1"

### Takeaway
Comprehension rises roughly **linearly** with the share of known words, with no sharp cliff. In practice, **~98% known tokens** (1 unknown word in 50) is the "comfortable / unassisted" target and **~95%** is the "minimal, with effort" floor. For the CSAT this means about **4,000 word families for 95%** and **about 7,000 for 98%** of reading-passage tokens (2020 CSAT analysis). Extensive reading works best for adults, but only with material at or near the 98% level, and "i+1" is a metaphor, not a measurable rule.

### Cited Findings
**Coverage thresholds**
- ✓ [B] (L2-adult/college) **Hu & Nation (2000)**, *Reading in a Foreign Language* 13(1). The authors took a 633-word fiction text and replaced low-frequency words with nonwords to create 80/90/95/100% known-word versions. They estimated that **98% coverage (≈1 unknown in 50 words)** is needed for most learners to reach adequate comprehension. At 90% only a small minority comprehended adequately, and at 95% most still fell short. Source: secondary summaries, [Kremmel et al. replication record](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/210922); [AILA 2021 abstract](https://aila2021.dryfta.com/program/abstract-archive/abstract/public/416/unknown-vocabulary-density-reading-comprehension-and-the-98-coverage-figure-replicating-hu-nation-2000)
- ✓ [B] **Replication caveat:** Kremmel and colleagues (2023, *Language Learning*), with **104 Sri Lankan adult EFL learners**, could **not fully replicate** Hu & Nation's results. A Victoria University of Wellington thesis reported errors in how earlier studies calculated optimum coverage and argued that much coverage research may really have been measuring vocabulary size. — [Lancaster eprint](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/210922); [VUW thesis](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Thresholds_Text_Coverage_Vocabulary_Size_and_Reading_Comprehension_in_Applied_Linguistics/17057906)
- ✓ [B] (L2-adult, 8 countries) **Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011)**, "The percentage of words known in a text and reading comprehension," *Modern Language Journal* 95(1), 26–43. The study found a **relatively linear relationship** between % vocabulary known and % comprehension and **no threshold** where comprehension jumps sharply. — [NAU record](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-percentage-of-words-known-in-a-text-and-reading-comprehension/)
- ✓ [D] Laufer's TESOL Quarterly review summarizes Schmitt et al. (2011) as recommending **98% coverage for academic texts if ~70% comprehension is expected**. — [Laufer, TQ 47(4) PDF](https://tcnewsletter.s3.amazonaws.com/all/newsmanager.commpartners.com/tesolc/downloads/TJ_TQ_Book%20Chapters/TQ_vol47-4_laufer.pdf)
- ✓ [C] (L2-adolescent/adult, Israel) **Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010)**, "Lexical threshold revisited," *Reading in a Foreign Language* 22(1), 15–30. They propose two thresholds. The **optimal** one is **8,000 word families → 98% coverage** (including proper nouns); the **minimal** one is **4,000–5,000 word families → 95% coverage**. Measures were the Levels Test, VocabProfile, and a national reading test. Laufer's 2021 reply describes 98% as optimal and 95% as minimal coverage, a figure that goes back to Laufer (1989), and estimates ~8,000–9,000 families for 98% in authentic texts. — [ScholarSpace record](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/eaead47e-853e-4797-ba6f-596df150c1d7); [Haifa CRIS](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/lexical-threshold-revisited-lexical-text-coverage-learners-vocabu/)

**Vocabulary size needed**
- ✓ [C] (corpus) **Nation (2006)**, "How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?" *Canadian Modern Language Review* 63(1), 59–81. At 98% coverage, written text needs **8,000–9,000 word families** and spoken text **6,000–7,000**. By genre: newspapers 8–9k, novels 9k, children's movies 7k, unscripted speech 6–7k. — [Nation 2006 PDF (VUW)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf)
- ✓ [B] (L2-adult) **van Zeeland & Schmitt (2012)** suggest **95% coverage may suffice for listening**, reachable with **2,000–3,000 word families**. Schmitt et al. (2015) call Nation's (2006) answer "rather daunting" and propose replications. — [Cambridge, Language Teaching replication](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)

**CSAT-specific (Korean corpus studies)**
- ✓ [C] (Korean HS/CSAT) A corpus analysis of the **2020학년도 CSAT** using AntWordProfiler with BNC lists found:
  - **Listening:** 95% of tokens covered by **2,000** word families, 98% by **4,000**.
  - **Reading:** 95% covered by **4,000** word families, 98% by **7,000**.
  - **EBS-linked materials** demanded **more** vocabulary than the CSAT itself.
  - Source: [KoreaScience JAKO202031458604052](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO202031458604052.do)
- ✓ [C] (Korean HS/CSAT) CSAT reading passages from **2015–2017 vs. 2018–2020** (before vs. after absolute grading) showed **no significant differences in tokens, types, or TTR**, but did differ in high-frequency-band and academic-word distributions. — [CAU ScholarWorks PDF](https://scholarworks.bwise.kr/cau/bitstream/2019.sw.cau/54159/1/%ec%a0%88%eb%8c%80%ed%8f%89%ea%b0%80%20%eb%8f%84%ec%9e%85%20%ec%a0%84%e2%a6%81%ed%9b%84%20%ec%88%98%ed%95%99%eb%8a%a5%eb%a0%a5%ec%8b%9c%ed%97%98%20%ec%98%81%ec%96%b4%20%ec%9d%bd%ea%b8%b0%20%ec%a7%80%eb%ac%b8%ec%9d%98%20%ec%96%b4%ed%9c%98%20%eb%b6%84%ec%84%9d%20%eb%b9%84%ea%b5%90.pdf)
- ✓ [C] (Korean HS/CSAT) **2018–2021 CSAT reading passages** showed no significant year-to-year differences across 25 lexical-complexity and 14 syntactic-complexity indices (LCA, L2SCA). In other words, passage complexity is fairly stable from year to year. — reported in [학습자중심교과교육연구 2022 (Kyobo Scholar)](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010028849862)
- ✓ [C] One textbook/EBS comparison study reported **2,897 (textbooks) and 3,323 (EBS)** word families needed for 95% coverage. It was not clear whether the CSAT itself was analyzed. — [Kyobo Scholar 4010023706816](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010023706816)

**Extensive reading and "i+1"**
- ✓ [A] (mixed ages, L2) **Jeon & Day (2016)**, "The effectiveness of ER on reading proficiency: A meta-analysis," *Reading in a Foreign Language* 28(2), 246–265. The analysis covers **49 studies / 71 samples / 5,919 participants (1980–2014)** and finds small-to-medium effects overall.
  - **By age:** **adults d ≈ 0.70**, children 0.52, adolescents 0.35.
  - **By setting and medium:** EFL > ESL, and web-based > paper.
  - **By outcome:** ≈0.47 for vocabulary and ≈0.54 for comprehension (as summarized secondarily).
  - **Program length:** no clear difference, but only 4 programs lasted longer than one year.
  - Source: [ScholarSpace](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899); [NFLRC](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/354)
- ◇ [D] **Krashen (1985)**, *The Input Hypothesis* (Longman). Acquisition comes from comprehensible input slightly beyond current competence ("i+1"). The construct has long been criticized as **not operationalizable or falsifiable** (e.g., McLaughlin 1987, *Theories of Second-Language Learning*). The coverage studies above are the measurable stand-in for "i+1". — [Krashen, Principles & Practice (author-hosted PDF)](http://www.sdkrashen.com/content/books/principles_and_practice.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Coverage converted to passage counts (arithmetic).** A typical CSAT reading passage is roughly 120–200 words; I did not verify this range this session.
  - At **98%**, a 150-word passage has **~3 unknown words**.
  - At **95%**, it has **~7–8**.
  - At **90%**, it has **~15**, which is "frustration level" for most readers, per Hu & Nation.
  - A tutor can calibrate by having the learner **underline unknown words on a first read** and counting them per 100 words: **≤2/100** suits fluency/extensive work, **3–5/100** suits intensive/exam practice, and **>5/100** is too hard for this learner now.
- **Linearity matters for scoring.** Because Schmitt et al. found no cliff, every extra 1% of coverage buys comprehension. Vocabulary growth therefore keeps paying off right up to the exam, rather than paying off only after crossing a threshold.
- **CSAT-specific vocabulary target.**
  - **Minimum:** ~**4,000 word families**, i.e. 95% of reading tokens. This is enough to *attempt* every passage.
  - **Comfortable:** ~**7,000 word families** (98%) for reading and ~4,000 for listening (98%), per the 2020 CSAT analysis.
  - Since English is absolute-graded (90+ = grade 1), a learner at ~95% coverage plus good strategy can plausibly reach grade 1 without the full 7,000. This is an inference: no study links CSAT English grades to vocabulary size.
- **EBS as the hard end.** EBS-linked books are lexically harder than the CSAT itself, so they are suitable as Level 4–5 material rather than entry material.
- **Two reading modes, two difficulty targets** (extrapolated from Hu & Nation plus Jeon & Day):
  - (a) **Fluency/extensive reading** at ≥98% coverage: graded readers or easy news, high volume. The strong adult ER effect (d ≈ 0.70) supports this.
  - (b) **Intensive exam practice** at 95–98% coverage, with glossing or post-reading vocabulary work.

### Gaps
- I could not get Schmitt et al.'s (2011) exact comprehension-by-coverage table (lextutor.ca is blocked in this session), so I cannot quote "X% coverage → Y% comprehension" beyond the "98% → ~70%" summary.
- I found no study linking CSAT English *grade* or *raw score* to vocabulary size or coverage.
- I found no verified CSAT item-type difficulty ranking (e.g., 빈칸추론/순서/문장삽입 being hardest) from KICE item statistics. This is widely believed among practitioners, but I did not confirm it with a primary source this session.
- Most coverage studies use fiction or general academic texts with university-level L2 adults. They are not directly about adult CSAT re-takers.

## Q2. Vocabulary learning: spaced retrieval, number of encounters, daily new-word load

### Takeaway
**Retrieval beats restudy** for word pairs, and **spacing beats massing**. Whether intervals should *expand* or stay *equal* matters much less, and the evidence on that is mixed. Incidental learning needs roughly **~10 encounters**, though the number varies widely (2–20+). Deliberate flashcard study is best done to a criterion of **about 3 correct recalls**, followed by **3+ spaced relearning sessions**. No study establishes an optimal number of new words per day, so any daily load is an extrapolation.

### Cited Findings
**Retrieval and spacing schedules**
- ✓ [B] (college, L2 word pairs) **Karpicke & Roediger (2008)**, "The critical importance of retrieval for learning," *Science* 319, 966–968, DOI [10.1126/science.1152408](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408).
  - **Design:** 40 undergraduates learned 40 Swahili–English pairs.
  - **Result:** conditions that kept testing items after they were first recalled reached **~80%** on a one-week delayed test, versus much lower when items were dropped from testing. Secondary sources say ~30–40%; that figure is unverified.
  - **Restudy:** once an item could be recalled, further *study* added no measurable learning, but further *testing* did.
  - **Replication:** Soderstrom, Kerr & Bjork replicated the core result.
  - Sources: [Purdue release](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html); [Soderstrom et al. PDF](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/11/soderstorm_kerr.pdf)
- ✓ [B] (L2-college, Japan) **Nakata (2015)**, "Effects of expanding and equal spacing on second language vocabulary learning," *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 37, 677–711, DOI [10.1017/S0272263114000825](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825).
  - **Design:** 128 students learned 20 English–Japanese pairs. The study crossed spacing type (expanding vs. equal) with spacing amount (massed / short / medium / long).
  - **Result:** expanding spacing had a **limited but significant advantage**, and *introducing* spacing at all had a **larger effect**. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-expanding-and-equal-spacing-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/D1D796306985C52F9BE7A1200AC50DB9)
- ✓ [D] **Nakata's later critical review** ("Gradually increasing spacing does not increase vocabulary learning") concludes that expanding spacing does not reliably aid long-term retention, that its pedagogical value for L2 vocabulary may be limited, and that Landauer & Bjork (1978) has methodological limits. — [Kansai University repository](https://kansai-u.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/10256)
- ◇ [B] (online adults, facts) **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008)**, "Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention," *Psychological Science* 19(11), 1095–1102, DOI [10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x).
  - The optimal gap between study sessions **grows with the retention interval**.
  - As a ratio of the retention interval it **shrinks**: about **20–40% for a 1-week test** down to about **5–10% for a 1-year test**.
- ◇ [B] (college) **Rawson & Dunlosky (2011)**, "Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning: How much is enough?" *JEP: General* 140(3), 283–302, DOI [10.1037/a0023956](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023956).
  - **Recommendation:** practice to **3 correct recalls** in the initial session, then **relearn in about 3 spaced sessions**.
  - **"Relearning-override" pattern:** after several relearning sessions, the initial criterion matters little.

**Number of encounters**
- ✓ [B] (L2-college, Japan) **Webb (2007)**, *Applied Linguistics* 28(1), 46–65. With 121 EFL learners, **considerable gains appear around 10 encounters in context**, and full knowledge of a word may need more. — summarized in [Uchihara et al. (2022) PDF (UCL)](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10154988/1/Uchihara%20et%20al.%20%282022%29%20Language%20Learning.pdf)
- ✓ [A] (L2, mixed) **Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019)**, "The effects of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis of correlational studies," *Language Learning* 69(3), DOI [10.1111/lang.12343](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/lang.12343).
  - Repetition has a **medium effect, r = .34**.
  - **Moderators:** learner age, vocabulary knowledge, spacing, visual support, engagement, and range of encounters.
  - **Prior knowledge:** knowing the most frequent 2,000 words **negatively moderated** the repetition effect.
  - Across studies, the number of encounters needed ranged from **2 to 20**.

**Knowledge "strength" as a difficulty ladder**
- ◇ [C] (L2-adolescent/adult, Israel) **Laufer & Goldstein (2004)**, "Testing vocabulary knowledge: Size, strength, and computer adaptiveness," *Language Learning* 54(3), 399–436, DOI [10.1111/j.0023-8333.2004.00260.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0023-8333.2004.00260.x). The study found an implicational hierarchy of difficulty. From easiest to hardest:
  1. passive recognition (choose the L1 meaning)
  2. active recognition (choose the L2 form)
  3. passive recall (supply the meaning)
  4. active recall (supply the L2 form)

  Passive recall was the best predictor of reading-related performance.

### Inferences
- **Schedule for a ~12-month horizon** (extrapolated from Cepeda et al. 2008 plus Rawson & Dunlosky 2011):
  - **Initial session:** retrieve each new word until it has been correctly recalled **3 times**.
  - **Reviews:** at about **1 day, 3–4 days, 1 week, 2–3 weeks, then roughly monthly** until the exam.
  - Whether the gaps expand or stay equal matters little (Nakata 2015 and his review). What matters is that the reviews are **spaced** and that each one is **retrieval** (recall before revealing), not rereading.
- **Item difficulty for vocabulary** follows Laufer & Goldstein's hierarchy:
  - **New words:** start with recognition (multiple-choice meaning).
  - **Once retrieval succeeds:** move to passive recall (L2 → Korean meaning, typed or spoken).
  - **Mastery check:** meaning-in-context within an unseen passage, which is the CSAT-relevant skill.
  - Active recall (Korean → English form) matters little for CSAT reading, which is receptive, but it helps the 어법/어휘 items.
- **Daily load (extrapolation, not tested).** Suppose the learner starts at ~2,000–3,000 word families and aims for ~4,000–5,000 (the 95% floor) by November 2027. That is ~1,500–2,500 families in ~52 weeks, about **30–50 per week net retained**. Allowing for forgetting and the fact that a "family" covers several forms, a deliberate study load of **~15–25 new items/day (5–6 days/week)** with spaced review is a reasonable starting point. Reaching 7,000 would roughly double this, which is ambitious alongside five other subjects.
- **Calibrate from data:** if the review backlog keeps growing, or same-day 3-correct recall takes too many trials, cut new items. If delayed recall is above ~90%, add items. (This links to the 85% target in other notes. Wilson et al. 2019, *Nature Communications* 10:4646 ◇ [doi:10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4), is a theoretical model, not a vocabulary study.)
- **Encounter budget:** reading alone gives too few repetitions for mid-frequency words. Because Webb (2007) puts the need at ~10 encounters, deliberate flashcard retrieval should *supplement* reading rather than replace it.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT on the **optimal number of new L2 words per day** for adults. Daily-load figures are extrapolations.
- I could not confirm the details of Nakata & Webb (2016, *SSLA*) on part vs. whole learning (set size) or Nakata (2017, *SSLA*) on within-session retrieval counts. Both are relevant to "how many cards per batch" and "how many retrievals per session" but were not opened this session.
- Kornell (2009, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*) on flashcard spacing vs. cramming did not come up in searches and was not verified.

## Q3. First-language reading (국어 독서/비문학): text complexity, background knowledge, cohesion, strategy instruction, Korean research

### Takeaway
Passage difficulty is a **reader × text × task** interaction, not a single readability number.
- **Background knowledge** can outweigh general reading skill (Recht & Leslie 1988).
- **Cohesion** helps low-knowledge readers but can *reduce* deep processing for high-knowledge readers (McNamara's reverse-cohesion effect).
- **Strategy instruction** gives only small gains on standardized measures.

Korean CSAT evidence points to the same drivers: unfamiliar domains (economics, law, science/tech), information density, conditions requiring calculation or multi-case reasoning, and item syntactic complexity.

### Cited Findings
**Background knowledge and cohesion**
- ✓ [B] (L1, grades 7–8) **Recht & Leslie (1988)**, "Effect of prior knowledge on good and poor readers' memory of text," *Journal of Educational Psychology* 80(1), 16–20.
  - **Design:** 64 students were crossed on reading ability and baseball knowledge. They read about half an inning and re-enacted it with models.
  - **Result:** high-knowledge **poor** readers outperformed low-knowledge **good** readers. Low-knowledge students did poorly regardless of reading skill.
  - **Caveat:** critics argue the design did not validly test the effect of reading skill.
  - Sources: [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_Study); [Shanahan on Literacy critique](https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/prior-knowledge-or-he-isnt-going-to-pick-on-the-baseball-study)
- ✓ [A−] (L1, mid–late primary children) **Smith, Snow, Serry & Hammond (2021)**, "The role of background knowledge in reading comprehension: A critical review," *Reading Psychology* 42(3), 214–240, DOI [10.1080/02702711.2021.1888348](https://doi.org/10.1080/02702711.2021.1888348). This is a systematic critical review of **23 studies**.
  - Background knowledge effects depend on the **nature of the text, the depth of situation model required, and reader misconceptions**.
  - **Low-knowledge readers benefit more from high-cohesion text.**
  - **Weaker readers partly compensate** when they have high knowledge.
  - Knowledge does not fully explain comprehension differences among accurate decoders.
  - Sources: [Open-access PDF (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/395416678.pdf); [T&F](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02702711.2021.1888348)
- ◇ [B] (L1, adolescents) **McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996)**, "Are good texts always better? Interactions of text coherence, background knowledge, and levels of understanding in learning from text," *Cognition and Instruction* 14(1), 1–43, DOI [10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1). This is the **reverse-cohesion effect**:
  - **Low-knowledge readers** learn more from high-cohesion text.
  - **High-knowledge readers** do better on inference/deep questions with **low-cohesion** text, because gaps force active bridging.
- ◇ [C] **McNamara, Graesser, McCarthy & Cai (2014)**, *Automated Evaluation of Text and Discourse with Coh-Metrix* (Cambridge UP). Coh-Metrix indexes include narrativity, syntactic simplicity, word concreteness, referential cohesion, and deep cohesion, and serve as multidimensional text-difficulty measures beyond traditional readability formulas. — [Cambridge UP](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511894664)
- ◇ [D] **Common Core State Standards, Appendix A (2010/2012 supplement)**. The "stretch" Lexile band for **grades 11–CCR is 1185–1385L**. It is a U.S. L1 benchmark; I found no 국어 equivalent. — [CCSS Appendix A supplement PDF](https://www.corestandards.org/assets/E0813_Appendix_A_New_Research_on_Text_Complexity.pdf)

**Strategy instruction**
- ✓ [A] (L1, whole classrooms, mixed ages) **Okkinga et al. (2018)**, "Effectiveness of reading-strategy interventions in whole classrooms: A meta-analysis," *Educational Psychology Review* 30, 1215–1239. Pooling **52 studies / 125 comparisons**, it found **d = .186 on standardized** comprehension tests and **d = .431 on researcher-made** tests. Programs looked most useful for **low-achieving adolescents**. — [Rotterdam UAS record](https://www.rotterdamuas.com/research/projects-and-publications/pub/effectiveness-of-reading-strategy-interventio/4c38634a-4354-4885-9093-6405172b969d/); [Springer PDF mirror](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-018-9445-7.pdf)
- ◇ [A] (L1, children/adolescents) **Rosenshine & Meister (1994)**, reciprocal teaching review, *Review of Educational Research* 64(4), 479–530, DOI [10.3102/00346543064004479](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064004479). The median effect was **~0.32 on standardized tests** and **~0.88 on experimenter-made tests**.

**Korean research and CSAT reports**
- ✓ [C] (Korean HS) A 수능 비문학 learner-perception study (*독서연구* 71호) found that students blame difficulty on:
  - **text length**
  - **unfamiliar vocabulary**
  - **the test situation**
  - **lack of background knowledge**
  - **excessive information load**
  - **(lack of) personal interest**

  The authors call for adjusting difficulty and reviewing topic selection. — [Record (UTB discovery mirror)](https://vufind.katalog.k.utb.cz/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-k558-4b2ffd407b8c337ecdaa24a39c31ab7a641e3735ffd707cc4c7d247ba9839ff03)
- ✓ [C] (Korean middle school) In a study of how students rate expository-text difficulty, genre and structure mattered, but so did **reader interest**. The authors conclude that **readability formulas alone cannot explain difficulty**. — [earticle A342600](https://www.earticle.net/Article/A342600)
- ✓ [C] (Korean; thesis) An Ewha dissertation proposes a **task-factor model** of reading difficulty that adds the task to text factors. It has three parts: the linguistic complexity of the item, its cognitive level, and the performance conditions (e.g., time). — [Ewha DSpace](https://dspace.ewha.ac.kr/handle/2015.oak/211220?mode=full)
- ✓ [C] (Korean CSAT 국어 독서) A regression study reported that **each 1-unit increase in mean item syntactic complexity → ~2.5 percentage-point lower 정답률** (statistically significant). *Link attribution uncertain:* this figure appeared in a search summary alongside [KoreaScience JAKO202016263392724](https://www.koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202016263392724.pdf), and the report writer should verify it before quoting.
- ✓ [D] (CSAT reports)
  - The **2019학년도** CSAT cut 인문·과학 지문 from ~2,200–2,300자 to ~1,500–1,600자, but kept discrimination power through information density. — [서울신문 2019-11-15](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/2019/11/15/20191115011004)
  - Teacher panels repeatedly cite **economics passages, passages requiring mathematical calculation, unfamiliar law/society concepts, and items requiring checking several cases/conditions** as high-difficulty. — [머니투데이 2020-12-03](https://www.mt.co.kr/policy/2020/12/03/2020120311453190067); [뉴시스 2022-11-17](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20221117_0002090038)
  - In the **2026학년도** CSAT, 독서 was relatively hard while 문학 and electives were easier. The stated direction was to avoid "excessive inference" and to keep the information needed to judge options **explicit in the passage**. — [뉴시스 2025-11-13](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20251113_0003401461)
  - EBS-linked concepts (e.g., retrovirus) were judged to lower the threshold for humanities-track students. — [nocutnews](https://nocutnews.co.kr/news/5659386)
- ✓ [C] Korean text-difficulty tools exist. **KReaD** (a 국어 text-difficulty program) has been validated against expert judgment with generally high reliability (search summary). TOPIK reading analyses use surface, lexical, syntactic, and discourse (referential cohesion, connectives) indices. — [Kyobo Scholar 4010070166685](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4010070166685)

### Inferences
- **The main lever for 국어 독서 practice-exam difficulty is topic familiarity × cohesion × information density × item task.** Character count is a weak proxy (the 2019 CSAT got shorter but not easier).
- **For a novice adult:**
  - Begin with **familiar domains and high-cohesion** texts, which Smith et al. and McNamara (1996) show low-knowledge readers need.
  - Then **hold the domain constant while lowering cohesion** or raising density.
  - Only then introduce **unfamiliar domains** (economics, law, science/tech), which Korean CSAT reports consistently flag as hardest.
- **Treat background knowledge as a curriculum, not a nuisance.** Recht & Leslie and Smith et al. imply that building domain schemata (basic economics, law, and science/tech concepts that recur in CSAT 독서) is a legitimate difficulty-reducer. Reading several passages within one domain is better than random topic hopping. This is an extrapolation; I found no Korean intervention study.
- **Strategy instruction (summarizing, structure mapping, questioning) should be brief.** Effects on standardized tests are small (d ≈ .19 to .32), so spend most time on reading volume and knowledge, not repeated strategy lessons.
- **Item-level difficulty is separate from passage difficulty.** Item syntactic complexity predicts 정답률, and so do multi-condition, calculation, and application items (〈보기〉 cases). A "just right" exam can pair an easier passage with a harder item type, or the reverse, to isolate the skill being trained.

### Gaps
- I found **no KCI study that systematically models CSAT 국어 독서 passage difficulty** with Coh-Metrix-style indices and item 정답률 in one design. This session saw only fragments.
- **No evidence was found for 문학 (literature) or 문법 (언어) item difficulty factors.** These need separate research.
- Most of the background-knowledge evidence is from **children**. Adult evidence (e.g., Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara 2009, *Learning and Instruction*) was not verified this session.
- No validated Korean Lexile-equivalent band for CSAT passages was found. The KReaD tool's CSAT-level scores were not retrieved.

## Q4. Mathematics problem difficulty: predictors, mixing routine/non-routine, productive struggle

### Takeaway
What makes a math problem hard is the **number of elements that must be processed simultaneously** (element interactivity) **relative to the learner's expertise**. Other drivers are the number of solution steps and subgoals, linguistic and representational complexity, and novelty (no known schema).

For **novices**, worked examples or explicit instruction come before independent problem solving. As expertise grows, guidance should fade (expertise reversal).

**Interleaving** problem types, where the learner must choose the strategy, produces large delayed gains. **Productive failure** (attempting a problem *before* instruction, followed by consolidation) shows sizable effects in a meta-analysis, but is contested for high-interactivity material.

### Cited Findings
**What predicts problem difficulty**
- ✓ [D/C review] (mostly children) **Daroczy, Wolska, Meurers & Nuerk (2015)**, "Word problems: A review of linguistic and numerical factors contributing to their difficulty," *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:348. It splits difficulty into three parts:
  - (i) the **linguistic complexity** of the text: word and sentence length, complex words, vocabulary, real-world knowledge;
  - (ii) the **numerical complexity**: number properties and the required operation;
  - (iii) the **relation between the two**, e.g. consistency of the wording with the operation.

  It calls for tightly controlled stimulus design. — [PMC4381502](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4381502)
- ✓ [B] (secondary students, geometry) **Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2015)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology*. Worked examples were better for **high element-interactivity** material, while **generating** answers was better for **low-interactivity** material. As expertise increased, the pattern changed. Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2017, *Educational Psychology Review*) argue the **expertise reversal effect is a special case of the element-interactivity effect**. — [EPR 2017 (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1)
- ✓ [D] **Kalyuga (2007)**, "Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction," *Educational Psychology Review* 19, 509–539. Guidance that helps novices becomes redundant or harmful for more expert learners. — [DOI 10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)
- ◇ [C] (adults, test items) **Embretson & Daniel (2008)**, *Psychology Science Quarterly* 50(3), 328–344. Cognitive-complexity features of math items, such as the number of equations or subgoals and the procedures required, predicted item difficulty. I did not verify this study this session; treat it as a lead.

**Explicit instruction vs. problem solving first**
- ✓ [A] (mixed, mostly secondary) **Sinha & Kapur (2021)**, "When problem solving followed by instruction works: Evidence for productive failure," *Review of Educational Research*, DOI [10.3102/00346543211019105](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105).
  - **Scope:** 53 studies / 166 comparisons of problem-solving-then-instruction (PS-I) vs. instruction-then-problem-solving (I-PS).
  - **Result:** a bias-corrected **g ≈ 0.87 favoring PS-I**, especially when the design is faithful to productive failure.
  - **Exceptions:** younger learners (grades 2–5) and domain-general skills favored I-PS (secondary report).
  - **Earlier review:** a pre-2015 review found ~0.43.
  - Source: [X-MOL abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1406031499744456704); [Tech&Learning summary](https://techlearning.com/news/study-productive-failure-a-success-in-education)
- ✓ [B] (secondary, math) **Ashman, Kalyuga & Sweller (2020, *Educational Psychology Review*)** found the opposite for **high element-interactivity** content: **explicit instruction before problem solving was better**. Together with Sinha & Kapur, this is a genuine conflict in the literature. — [Springer PDF mirror](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-019-09500-5.pdf)
- ◇ [B] (college) **Renkl & Atkinson (2003)**, "Structuring the transition from example study to problem solving in cognitive skill acquisition," *Educational Psychologist* 38(1), 15–22, DOI [10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_3). **Backward fading** of worked-example steps smooths the shift from examples to independent problem solving.

**Interleaving**
- ✓ [B+] (grade 7, U.S.) **Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020)**, "A randomized controlled trial of interleaved mathematics practice," *Journal of Educational Psychology* 112(1), 40–52, DOI [10.1037/edu0000367](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1037/edu0000367).
  - **Design:** a preregistered cluster RCT with 54 classes, 787 students, and 15 teachers.
  - **Result:** on an unannounced test one month later, **interleaved 61% vs. blocked 38%, d = 0.83** (95% CI ≈ 0.68–0.97).
  - **Caveats:** the gain per unit of time may be smaller, and the trial covered one district only.
  - Sources: [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/efficacy-study-interleaved-mathematics-practice); [Structural Learning summary](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/mix-it-up)
- ◇ [B] (college) **Rohrer & Taylor (2007)**, "The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning," *Instructional Science* 35, 481–498, DOI [10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8). Volume-of-solids problems, one week later: **shuffled ≈63% vs. blocked ≈20%**. Practice performance was *worse* under shuffling, which is the classic desirable-difficulty pattern.

**CSAT context**
- ✓ [D] (CSAT context) For the **2024학년도** CSAT, the exam chair said so-called **"killer items" (초고난도 문항)** were excluded, yet EBS teachers judged items to still be hard. Common subjects tended to be harder than electives. — [세계일보 2023-11-16](https://www.segye.com/view/20231116524498); [데일리안](https://www.dailian.co.kr/news/view/1174491/)

### Inferences
- **Operational difficulty factors for a CSAT math item** (synthesis of the above; the weights are judgment, not estimated):
  1. **Element interactivity / concepts combined:** one concept, two from the same unit, or cross-unit (e.g., 수열 + 함수, 미분 + 그래프 개형).
  2. **Solution length:** the number of steps or subgoals.
  3. **Strategy selection:** whether the item type is cued (blocked) or must be identified (interleaved / unlabeled).
  4. **Representation:** symbolic only, or translation from a graph, table, or word context.
  5. **Novelty:** a familiar schema, or a novel condition such as case analysis or 조건 해석 for "functions satisfying (가)(나)".
  6. **Computation load:** arithmetic burden and error opportunities.
- **Sequencing for a novice adult** (resolving the Sinha & Kapur vs. Ashman et al. conflict by element interactivity; this is an inference):
  - For **new, high-interactivity topics** (e.g., 미적분I limits/derivatives), use worked example → faded example → similar problem.
  - For topics where the learner already has the component skills, a short **productive-failure** attempt before the explanation is reasonable, *provided* a consolidation and explanation phase follows.
- **Interleave once basics are in place.** Practice sets should become **mixed / unlabeled** soon after each type has been introduced. Expect **lower practice scores but higher delayed scores** (Rohrer & Taylor 2007; Rohrer et al. 2020), so do not "correct" difficulty downward just because interleaved sets feel harder.
- **Proportions of routine vs. non-routine items (extrapolation).**
  - **Early phase:** mostly routine, single-concept items at the 2–3점 level, plus 1–2 stretch items per set.
  - **Middle phase:** ~60/30/10 routine / multi-concept / novel.
  - **Final 2–3 months:** full-length mocks matching the CSAT mix.
  - Since killer items are officially excluded, high-difficulty practice should focus on **multi-concept 4점 items** rather than puzzle-like extremes.

### Gaps
- I found **no KCI study quantifying CSAT 수학 item-difficulty predictors** (steps, concept count, computation) against 정답률. The only related model was for another CSAT subject (농업 이해: content area, behavior domain, concept, item format). — [Kyobo Scholar 4050028130283](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4050028130283)
- Turner, Dossey, Blum & Niss (2013) on PISA mathematical-competency ratings predicting item difficulty is likely relevant but was not verified this session.
- The productive-failure moderators for adult novices are unclear. The meta-analysis moderator details came from a secondary page.
- Evidence on the "productive struggle" construct in the mathematics-education sense (e.g., Warshauer 2015, *JMTE*) was not retrieved.

## Q5. Content subjects (통합과학 / 통합사회 / 한국사): retrieval practice, concept maps, item-type ladder (Bloom revised)

### Takeaway
Classroom quizzing on science content produces durable gains, including on **new, application-type exam items** (McDaniel et al. 2011, 2013). Retrieval practice also beats concept mapping, even on inference tests (Karpicke & Blunt 2011).

Practice should **match the cognitive level of the target test**. Fact-only quizzes improve fact tests but **do not** by themselves raise higher-order performance; higher-order or **mixed** quizzes do (Agarwal 2019).

Bloom's revised taxonomy (Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create; Anderson & Krathwohl 2001) gives a defensible item-difficulty ladder. CSAT-style data and source-interpretation items sit at Apply and Analyze.

### Cited Findings
- ✓ [B] (L1, grade 8 science, classroom) **McDaniel, Agarwal, Huelser, McDermott & Roediger (2011)**, "Test-enhanced learning in a middle school science classroom: The effects of quiz frequency and placement," *Journal of Educational Psychology* 103(2), 399–414, DOI [10.1037/a0021782](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021782). Low-stakes quizzing improved unit and end-of-semester exam performance. The press summary reports gains even on **exam items students had never seen**. — [WashU Source](https://source.washu.edu/2011/03/quizzes-key-to-learning-for-middle-school-students/); [Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jedup/pdf/10.1037/a0021782~test-enhanced-learning-in-a-middle-school-science-classroom)
- ✓ [B] (L1, middle-school science, classroom) **McDaniel, Thomas, Agarwal, McDermott & Roediger (2013)**, "Quizzing in middle-school science: Successful transfer performance on classroom exams," *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 27(3), 360–372. Quizzing benefits transferred to exam questions that were worded differently or required application. — [Record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_1627949196)
- ✓ [B] (college; 200 students; science texts) **Karpicke & Blunt (2011)**, "Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping," *Science* 331, 772–775.
  - Retrieval practice beat concept mapping on **verbatim and inference** questions, **even when the final test required drawing concept maps**.
  - Students wrongly predicted that concept mapping would work better.
  - Source: [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577217); [Purdue release](https://www.purdue.edu/newsroom/research/2011/110120KarpickeScience.html)
- ✓ [B] (college ×2 experiments, grade 6 classroom ×1) **Agarwal (2019)**, "Retrieval practice & Bloom's taxonomy: Do students need fact knowledge before higher order learning?" *Journal of Educational Psychology* 111(2), 189–209.
  - **Fact quizzes** improved fact tests but **not** higher-order tests.
  - **Higher-order and mixed quizzes** improved higher-order tests.
  - Final performance was best when **practice matched the test's cognitive complexity**.
  - In Experiment 2, **mixed quizzes outperformed higher-order-only** quizzes on higher-order tests.
  - Source: [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/27-1); [RetrievalPractice.org](https://retrievalpractice.org/blooms)
- ◇ [D] **Anderson & Krathwohl (Eds.) (2001)**, *A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing* (Longman). It separates a cognitive-process dimension (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) from a knowledge dimension (Factual, Conceptual, Procedural, Metacognitive). Overview: **Krathwohl (2002)**, *Theory Into Practice* 41(4), 212–218, DOI [10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2).
- ◇ [A] **Pan & Rickard (2018)**, "Transfer of test-enhanced learning: Meta-analytic review and synthesis," *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7), 710–756, DOI [10.1037/bul0000151](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000151). Retrieval practice transfers to new question types/contexts with a moderate effect (~d 0.40). Transfer is stronger with **elaborative feedback** and when initial practice requires **broader retrieval**. Exact figures not verified this session.
- ◇ [A] **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017)**, "Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing," *Review of Educational Research* 87(3), 659–701, DOI [10.3102/0034654316689306](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654316689306). The overall practice-testing advantage is moderate (~g 0.6), and the effect holds across ages and formats. Exact figures not verified this session.
- ✓ [C] (Korean CSAT, other subject) An item-difficulty study of CSAT 「농업 이해」 found that **content area, behavioral domain (cognitive level), subject concept, and item format** jointly drive difficulty. This is the closest Korean CSAT analogue for content subjects. — [Kyobo Scholar 4050028130283](https://scholar.kyobobook.co.kr/article/detail/4050028130283)

### Inferences
- **For 통합과학, 통합사회, and 한국사**, practice items should move from **fact recall → concept explanation and classification → application to a new scenario → interpretation of data or sources (자료/사료/그래프/실험 결과) → multi-unit integration**.
- **Do not wait for fact mastery before giving higher-order items.** Agarwal (2019) shows fact quizzes alone do not build higher-order performance and that *mixed* quizzes work best. From early on, each practice set should include some application or data items, not only recall.
- **Concept maps can be kept, but as a retrieval exercise.** The learner draws the map *from memory*, then checks it against the source. Studying with the source open is the weaker form (Karpicke & Blunt 2011).
- **한국사** (absolute grading; most universities only require a minimum grade) is largely Remember/Understand: chronology, cause–effect, and source identification. It can stay at Levels 1–3 for most of the year, with Level 4 source-interpretation mocks near the exam. Whether this calibration is enough depends on the learner's target grade; the CSAT 한국사 grading scheme was not verified this session.
- **통합과학 / 통합사회** are new as CSAT subjects for 2028, so there are no past CSAT items. KICE released sample/예시 items for the 2028 reform (not retrieved this session); use them to anchor Levels 4–5.

### Gaps
- I found no study of retrieval practice with **adult novices** on **Korean social-studies or history** content specifically.
- KICE's **2028 통합사회/통합과학 예시 문항** and their published difficulty data were not retrieved.
- I found no study directly validating "Bloom level" as a *psychometric* predictor of item difficulty. Bloom levels correlate with difficulty but are not identical to it; a recall item about an obscure fact can be harder than an application item.

## Q6. Proposed 5-level difficulty ladders per subject (grounded in the above; extrapolations marked)

### Takeaway
Each ladder below changes **one or two evidence-backed difficulty drivers per level**:
- **English:** lexical coverage and item type
- **국어:** topic familiarity, cohesion and density, item task
- **Math:** element interactivity, steps, strategy cueing, novelty
- **Content subjects:** Bloom level and data or multi-unit integration

The cut-points are **extrapolations (E)** unless tied to a specific citation. Promote a learner when accuracy on the current level is consistently high (target ~80–90% on delayed or mixed sets; see the companion notes on optimal success rates) and demote when it falls well below. These promotion rules are an extrapolation.

### Cited Findings
- English coverage anchors: 98% / 95% — [Hu & Nation 2000 via Lancaster](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/210922); [Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/eaead47e-853e-4797-ba6f-596df150c1d7). CSAT 2020 needs 4,000 WF (95%) and 7,000 WF (98%) — [KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO202031458604052.do). EBS is harder than the CSAT — same source.
- Vocabulary item hierarchy (recognition < recall) — [Laufer & Goldstein 2004 ◇](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0023-8333.2004.00260.x). Spacing and retrieval — [Karpicke & Roediger 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408); [Nakata 2015](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263114000825).
- 국어: background knowledge × cohesion — [Smith et al. 2021](https://core.ac.uk/download/395416678.pdf); [McNamara et al. 1996 ◇](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1). CSAT hard-passage features — [머니투데이](https://www.mt.co.kr/policy/2020/12/03/2020120311453190067); [뉴시스 2025](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20251113_0003401461).
- Math: element interactivity and expertise reversal — [Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller 2017](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1); interleaving — [Rohrer et al. 2020](https://zendy.io/pdf-viewer/10.1037/edu0000367); productive failure — [Sinha & Kapur 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105).
- Content: matched or mixed complexity — [Agarwal 2019](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/27-1); retrieval beats concept maps — [Karpicke & Blunt 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3577217); transfer from quizzes — [McDaniel et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021782).

### Inferences
**영어 독해 (English reading).** Coverage is checked by counting the learner's self-marked unknown words per 100.

| Level | Text (coverage for *this* learner) | Item types (E) | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ≥98% known (≤2 unknown/100 words); graded or easy expository text | Literal: 내용 일치, 도표, 안내문 | Untimed; gloss allowed |
| 2 | ~98%; CSAT-format passages on everyday topics | 목적·심경·요지·주제 | ~2 min/item soft limit |
| 3 | 96–98%; CSAT academic passages | 제목·함축 의미·어법·어휘 (문맥상 낱말) | Timed sections |
| 4 | ~95% (≈5 unknown/100); CSAT and EBS-linked academic passages | 순서·문장 삽입·요약문 | Strict timing |
| 5 | ≤95% / EBS-hard abstract passages | High-difficulty 빈칸 추론; full 45-item mock incl. listening | Full 70-min mock |

The item-type ordering is practitioner consensus and was not verified against KICE statistics (see Q1 Gaps). Listening can be anchored at ~95% coverage with ~2,000–3,000 WF (van Zeeland & Schmitt 2012; CSAT 2020 listening 95% = 2,000 WF).

**영어 어휘 (vocabulary items).**

| Level | Task |
|---|---|
| 1 | New word: recognition (multiple-choice Korean meaning) |
| 2 | Passive recall, decontextualized (English → Korean), to 3 correct recalls the same day |
| 3 | Passive recall after a ≥1-week delay (spaced retrieval) |
| 4 | Meaning in a new sentence context; word-family forms (derivatives); polysemy |
| 5 | Meaning in an unseen authentic CSAT/EBS passage; 문맥상 낱말 쓰임 judgment |

Levels 1–3 follow Laufer & Goldstein, Rawson & Dunlosky, and Cepeda; Levels 4–5 are E.

**국어 독서 (Korean expository reading).**

| Level | Passage | Item task | Conditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Familiar domain (humanities/everyday society), high cohesion, ~1,000–1,300자 | Explicit-information 일치/불일치 | Untimed |
| 2 | Familiar domain, CSAT length (~1,500–1,800자), normal cohesion | Connecting information across paragraphs; 핵심 개념 이해 | ~8–10 min/set |
| 3 | **Unfamiliar** domain (science/tech, economics) but high cohesion and EBS-linked concepts | Inference; cause–effect; comparing viewpoints | Timed |
| 4 | Unfamiliar domain, high information density or low cohesion | 〈보기〉 application to new cases; multi-condition judgment | Strict timing |
| 5 | Paired or 융합 passages; law/economics with calculation or case analysis | Highest syntactic-complexity items; multi-case reasoning | Full 80-min 국어 mock |

Grounding: Recht & Leslie; Smith et al. 2021; McNamara 1996; CSAT reports; the item-syntax regression. Character counts and timings are E. 문학 and 언어 ladders are not evidence-backed here (see Q3 Gaps).

**수학 (대수·미적분I·확률과 통계).**

| Level | Element interactivity / steps | Strategy cue | Representation / novelty | Format |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One concept, 1–2 steps | Blocked; immediately after a worked example | Symbolic, routine | 2점-type |
| 2 | One concept, 3–4 steps | Blocked, faded examples | Minor surface variation | 3점-type |
| 3 | Two concepts within a unit | **Interleaved / unlabeled** | Graph/table/word translation | 3–4점 |
| 4 | Cross-unit, 5+ steps or case analysis | Interleaved | Non-routine conditions ((가)(나) 조건), function-graph reasoning | 4점 multi-concept |
| 5 | Novel combinations | Mixed full mock | Unfamiliar framing | Highest 4점 items; full 100-min mock |

Grounding: element interactivity and expertise reversal, Rohrer interleaving, Renkl & Atkinson fading. Point mapping and step counts are E. For **new high-interactivity topics**, enter at Level 1 with worked examples (Ashman et al. 2020). For topics whose components the learner already knows, a short pre-instruction attempt at Level 3 is a defensible productive-failure move (Sinha & Kapur 2021) if a consolidation phase follows.

**통합과학 / 통합사회 / 한국사.**

| Level | Bloom level (Anderson & Krathwohl) | Example item |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Remember (factual) | Term or event recall; short answer or flashcard |
| 2 | Understand (conceptual) | Classify, compare, explain; 5-choice with plausible distractors |
| 3 | Apply | Use a concept or principle on a new scenario (e.g., predict a reaction outcome, apply an economic or legal principle) |
| 4 | Analyze | Interpret data, experiments, graphs, statistics, or 사료; identify the variable or era |
| 5 | Analyze/Evaluate across units | Multi-unit integrated items with data; full timed mock |

Grounding: Agarwal 2019 (start mixing Levels 1–2 with 3–4 early, not strictly sequentially); McDaniel 2011/2013 (quizzing transfers to application items); Karpicke & Blunt (concept maps from memory only). Level boundaries are E. 한국사 can be weighted toward Levels 1–3 plus 사료 interpretation (E).

**Cross-subject promotion rule (E).**
- **Promote** after two consecutive sessions at ≥85–90% on *delayed or mixed* items at the current level.
- **Hold** at 70–85%.
- **Demote**, or add worked examples and background-knowledge pre-reading, below ~60–70%.
- **Do not demote on interleaved math sets just because practice accuracy falls.** The interleaving literature predicts lower practice and higher delayed scores (Rohrer & Taylor 2007).

### Gaps
- None of these ladders has been validated empirically; they are research-informed designs.
- The numeric cut-points (character counts, step counts, accuracy bands) are extrapolations. Only the English coverage cut-points (95/98%) have direct empirical anchors.
- There are no CSAT 2028 (new curriculum) item statistics yet. Calibration should be revisited once KICE releases 예시/모의평가 data for the 2028 system. The 2027 June and September 모의평가 should be the first real anchors.
