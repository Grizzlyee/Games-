## Q1. Retrieval practice / testing effect: size, dependence on retrieval success and feedback, math vs factual recall

### Takeaway
Practice testing reliably beats restudy, by about g ≈ 0.5 in lab meta-analyses and about g ≈ 0.5 (≈0.43 after bias correction) in classroom quizzing across 48k students. The benefit grows with longer delays and with feedback. When initial retrieval fails and no feedback is given, the benefit is weak. For complex math problem solving the testing effect is **contested**: probably smaller than for factual or verbal recall, so worked examples and feedback matter more there.

### Cited Findings
**Foundational primary studies (lab, adult undergraduates, verbal prose)**
- **Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Psychological Science* 17(3):249–255, "Test-enhanced learning."** Study–test beat study–study at delays of 2 days and 1 week, but restudy won on a 5-minute test (the crossover). Search snippets give 1-week recall of **56% (one recall test) vs 42% (restudy), d ≈ 0.83** for Exp. 1 (N=120), and **61% (STTT) vs 40% (SSSS)** for Exp. 2. These are two different experiments, not conflicting reports. [B; lab; adults; verbal prose] — [Search summary of R&K 2006 (studycardsai, secondary)](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics); paper DOI [10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)
- **Karpicke & Blunt (2011), *Science* 331:772–775.** Retrieval practice beat elaborative concept mapping on a 1-week test of science text, including inference questions: **~67% vs 45%, d ≈ 1.50** (secondary report). Students *predicted* that retrieval practice would be the worst method, a metacognitive illusion. Exp. 2 had 120 Purdue undergraduates. [B; lab; adults; science prose, verbatim + inference] — [Karpicke & Blunt supporting material (Purdue lab)](https://pcllab.github.io/downloads/2011/2011_Karpicke_Blunt_ScienceSupportingMaterial.pdf); [scite report](https://scite.ai/reports/retrieval-practice-produces-more-learning-yejga6); DOI [10.1126/science.1199327](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327)

**Meta-analyses**
- **Rowland (2014), *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6):1432–1463, DOI 10.1037/a0037559.** Overall testing vs restudy **g ≈ 0.50 (159 effect sizes, 61 studies; secondary count)**. Initial *recall* tests give larger benefits than recognition tests, which supports an effortful-processing account. The effect is larger when the final test is ≥1 day later (**g = 0.69**) than same-day (**g = 0.41**). The effect is larger with feedback (reported **g ≈ 0.73 with feedback vs 0.39 without**: secondary blog figure, not checked against the paper). [A for direction; D for exact numbers; mostly lab, adults, verbal] — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/); [Loterre subject-index summary](https://data.loterre.fr/P66/en/page/-JRJHZ858-D); [secondary figures](https://studycardsai.com/blog/active-recall-statistics)
- **Rowland (2014), retrieval-success moderator.** In no-feedback studies, the testing effect grew with the initial test success rate and was small or unreliable when initial performance was low. ⚠ unverified: direction recalled, exact cut-points and g values not checked. [D] — [Rowland 2014 PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25150680/)
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), *Review of Educational Research* 87(3):659–701, DOI 10.3102/0034654316689306.** 118 studies. Practice tests beat restudy *and* all other comparison conditions. Effect sizes depended on test features, participant and study characteristics, outcome types, and methods. Feedback amplified the effect for some learners. [A; mixed lab + classroom; ages from elementary to postsecondary] — [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842); [Klingenstein summary](https://klingensteincenter.org/klingbrief/standardizing-retrieval)
- **Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021), *Psychological Bulletin* 147(4):399–435, DOI 10.1037/bul0000309.** **222 classroom studies, 573 effect sizes, ~48,478 students. Overall g = 0.499 (≈0.427 bias-corrected).** Moderators: the control condition's strategy, consistency between quiz and test format, matching of material, **corrective feedback**, **number of test repetitions**, test location and timepoint, treatment duration, and design. The effect held in primary, secondary, and postsecondary education. Proposed mechanisms: extra exposure, transfer-appropriate processing, and motivation. [A; **classroom**; children + adults; many subjects] — [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/); [x-mol abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1390918175120052224); [Research Bite summary](https://tipsforteachers.substack.com/p/research-bite-4-testing-quizzing)
- **Pan & Rickard (2018), *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7):710–756, DOI 10.1037/bul0000151, "Transfer of test-enhanced learning."** Grand mean transfer effect **d ≈ 0.40** (secondary). Transfer to new items or formats often **disappeared when the favorable conditions were absent**: response congruency between practice and final test, elaborated retrieval or feedback, and **high initial test performance**. These fit a three-factor framework. [A for direction; D for exact d] — [Pan lab accepted manuscript](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018W.pdf); [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zC1oBp6yE72b7YHzaJZmjXf)

**Expectation of later tests**
- **Szpunar, McDermott & Roediger (2007), *Memory & Cognition* 35:1007–1013, DOI 10.3758/BF03193473.** Initial testing helped final performance more when learners **expected a final cumulative test**. Proposed mechanism: the expectation prompts continued processing of the material after the initial test. [C; lab; adults; word lists] — [PubMed 17910184](https://www.pubmed.gov/17910184/)

**Math / complex problem solving (boundary condition)**
- **van Gog & Sweller (2015), *Educational Psychology Review* 27(2):247–264, DOI 10.1007/s10648-015-9310-x.** They argue that the testing effect **decreases or disappears as element interactivity (complexity) rises**, which bears on many school tasks. **Leahy, Hanham & Sweller (2015)** found an *inverted* testing effect on immediate tests and a non-significant positive effect at 1 week. [B for "smaller for complex material"; contested] — [EUR repository](https://pure.eur.nl/en/publications/not-new-but-nearly-forgotten-the-testing-effect-decreases-or-even/); [Didau summary of the debate](https://learningspy.co.uk/featured/the-testing-effect-is-dead-long-live-the-testing-effect)
- **Rebuttals in the same issue.** Karpicke & Aue (2015), "The testing effect is alive and well with complex materials," argue that "complexity / element interactivity" has no quantitative metric. Rawson (2015), "Still a Winner," argues that the cited studies show small *positive* effects. Roelle & Berthold (2017) attribute the nulls to non-equivalent knowledge construction across conditions. [B; contested] — [Springer issue 27(2)](https://www.springer.com/journal/10648/volumes-and-issues/27-2); [Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zMjGz9ddgwjxadBRxQCnnWY)
- **Expertise reversal.** Worked examples help novices, but the benefit reverses as expertise grows, at which point solving problems (retrieval) is better. ⚠ unverified detail. [B] — Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), *Educational Psychologist* 38(1):23–31, [DOI 10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_4)

### Inferences
- **Rule 1.1 (strong evidence):** Make **practice tests the main study activity**, not rereading. The classroom effect of ~0.5 SD (Yang 2021) is the realistic expectation for an adult studying course-like material.
- **Rule 1.2 (strong evidence):** **Always give corrective feedback.** After every practice exam, check answers and study the full worked solution for every miss *and* every lucky guess. Feedback is a major moderator in Rowland, Adesope, and Yang, and it is the main protection when initial retrieval fails.
- **Rule 1.3 (moderate evidence + extrapolation):** Calibrate difficulty so that retrieval mostly succeeds on review items, about **70–90% correct** on spaced-review sections. Use lower success rates (≈50–70%) only where feedback is guaranteed. This follows from the low-success / no-feedback weakness (Rowland) and from the transfer finding that high initial performance is a favorable condition (Pan & Rickard). The 70–90% band is an extrapolation and matches the FSRS 0.85–0.90 target retention (Q3).
- **Rule 1.4 (math-specific, moderate):** For a **newly met** math or science problem type, study 1–2 worked examples first, then do problems with feedback (expertise reversal + van Gog & Sweller). Once a type has been solved correctly at least once, switch to pure retrieval (solving without hints) in later spaced and interleaved sets. Do not expect lab-sized verbal testing effects (d ≈ 0.8–1.5) for multi-step 수학 킬러 problems. Expect a smaller but real benefit, most of it via spacing and interleaving (Q2, Q4).
- **Rule 1.5:** Tell yourself every chapter will appear again on a cumulative test (Szpunar 2007 expectancy effect). For 수능 this is literally true.
- The R&K 2006 crossover means a practice exam taken right after restudy will show *lower* scores under retrieval than under rereading. Judge strategies by scores ≥1 week later, never by same-day scores.

### Gaps
- Exact Rowland (2014) moderator values for initial-retrieval-success bins and the feedback × success interaction could not be verified, because the full text was blocked.
- The Adesope et al. (2017) pooled effect size could not be verified. I recall overall **g ≈ 0.61** and **≈0.51 vs restudy**, but these are ⚠ unverified. The breakdowns by test format (MC vs short answer vs mixed) and by setting were also not obtained.
- Yang et al. (2021) moderator values (e.g., g by number of quizzes, by feedback, by STEM vs non-STEM) were not obtained.
- No meta-analytic estimate of the testing effect *specifically for adult math problem solving* was found. The math evidence is a contested debate among a small number of experiments.
- I found no evidence specific to Korean-language reading comprehension (국어 비문학), where the skill is mainly inferential reading rather than retrieval of facts. Applying the testing effect there is an extrapolation.

## Q2. Spacing: optimal gap as a function of retention interval; implications for a ~12-month horizon; expanding vs equal intervals

### Takeaway
The best gap between learning sessions grows with the retention interval (RI), but as a *fraction* of the RI it shrinks: about **20–40% of a 1-week RI** down to about **5–10% of a 1-year RI** (Cepeda et al. 2008). For an exam ~12 months away, that means a first re-test gap of roughly **3–5 weeks** for material learned now. Gaps that are too short cost more than gaps that are too long. Expanding and equal intervals give **the same final retention** (Latimier 2021, g = 0.034). Expanding schedules keep recall higher *during* training (Kang 2014), which matters when practice exams double as progress checks.

### Cited Findings
- **Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(3):354–380, "Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks."** **317 experiments from 184 articles, 839 assessments.** Spaced beats massed. The interstudy interval that maximizes retention **increases as the RI increases**. Expanding intervals were also reviewed. [A; mostly lab; mostly adults; **verbal recall only**] — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1771/); [author PDF (Vul lab)](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf); [PubMed 16719566](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)
- **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), *Psychological Science* 19(11):1095–1102, "Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention."** At each test delay, a longer gap first increased and then gradually reduced final performance. The optimal gap increased with test delay, **but the optimal gap as a proportion of the test delay fell from ~20–40% at a 1-week delay to ~5–10% at a 1-year delay.** There were 26 gap × delay conditions. [A-/B; large online study; adults; verbal (trivia facts)] — [PubMed 19076480](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [eScholarship preprint](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0kp5q19x)
- **Cepeda 2008, design details.** ~1,350 participants learning 32 obscure trivia facts. Gaps ranged from 0 to ~3.5 months and test delays were 7, 35, 70, and 350 days. The optimal gap for the 350-day delay was ~3 weeks, and for the 70-day delay also ~3 weeks. ⚠ unverified: recalled from the paper; the full text was blocked. — [PubMed 19076480](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- **Asymmetry.** Gaps that are too long cost much less than gaps that are too short, so when unsure, err longer. ⚠ unverified: recalled from Pashler, Rohrer, Cepeda & Carpenter (2007), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 14(2):187–193, "Enhancing learning and retarding forgetting: Choices and consequences" ([DOI 10.3758/BF03194050](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03194050)). It is also consistent with the "gradual" decline described in the Cepeda 2008 abstract ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)).
- **Cepeda et al. (2009), *Experimental Psychology* 56(4):236–246, "Optimizing distributed practice: Theoretical analysis and practical implications."** Extended the gap × RI mapping, including a ~6-month RI with an optimal gap of about 1 month. ⚠ unverified: specific values recalled. — [Mozer lab reprint](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/Cepedaetal2009.pdf)
- **Math spacing:** Rohrer & Taylor (2006), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 20:1209–1224. Spreading the same math practice across two sessions about a week apart beat massing it on a delayed test. Extra same-session practice (**overlearning**) gave no benefit at a 4-week delay. ⚠ unverified: exact percentages not checked. [C; lab; undergraduates; math] — [DOI 10.1002/acp.1266](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.1266)
- **Karpicke & Roediger (2007), *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 33(4):704–719, "Expanding retrieval practice promotes short-term retention, but equally spaced retrieval enhances long-term retention."** The title states the result. The key driver was the delay before the *first* retrieval, which favors a non-immediate first test. ⚠ details unverified. [C; lab; adults; verbal] — [DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.33.4.704](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.33.4.704)
- **Kang, Lindsey, Mozer & Pashler (2014), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 21(6):1544–1550.** Over **multi-day** schedules, expanding and equal-interval retrieval practice gave **equivalent recall at an 8-week final test**. Expanding gave **much higher average recallability during the training period**. Earlier null results mostly came from single-session comparisons. [C; lab; adults; verbal/foreign vocabulary] — [Mozer lab PDF](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/KangLindseyMozerPashler2014.pdf); [CU Experts record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_95263)
- **Latimier, Peyre & Ramus (2021), *Educational Psychology Review* 33 (pages ⚠ unverified), DOI 10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8.** **Spaced vs massed retrieval practice: g = 0.74** (subset 1, 39 effect sizes). **Expanding vs uniform spacing: g = 0.034, non-significant** (subset 2, 54 effect sizes). The data support spacing retrieval out but **not** the belief that intervals must expand. [A; mostly lab; mostly adults; mostly verbal] — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8); [x-mol abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1340395608147148800)
- **Successive relearning (Rawson, Dunlosky & Sciartelli 2013, *Educational Psychology Review* 25:523–548).** First practice until correct recall, then relearn to one correct recall in several later spaced sessions. This gave large durable gains for course material, with sharply diminishing returns after ~3 relearning sessions. ⚠ unverified: details recalled. [B; classroom/lab; undergraduates; course concepts] — [DOI 10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4)

### Inferences
- **Gap ladder (EXTRAPOLATION from Cepeda 2008/2009).** Only the 1-week and 1-year endpoints are verified; the middle rows are interpolated. These are gaps between first learning and the first spaced re-test:

  | Material learned in… | RI to 수능 (approx.) | Suggested first re-test gap | Basis |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Months 1–3 (Nov 2026–Jan 2027) | 10–12.5 mo | **3–5 weeks** | 5–10% of ~1 yr (verified ratio) |
  | Months 4–6 | 7–9 mo | **3–4 weeks** | ~1 mo optimum at 6-mo RI (⚠ Cepeda 2009, recalled) |
  | Months 7–9 | 3–6 mo | **2–3 weeks** | ~3 wk optimum at 70-d RI (⚠ recalled) |
  | Months 10–11 | 1–2.5 mo | **5–10 days** | ratio rises toward 20–40% at short RIs (verified endpoint) |
  | Final 3–4 weeks | ≤1 mo | **2–5 days** | 20–40% of a 1–3 wk RI |

- **Rule 2.1:** Since too-short gaps hurt more than too-long ones, **round gaps up** when the calendar forces a choice.
- **Rule 2.2:** Do not re-test new material on the *same* day as the main assessment of its value. A quick same-day check is fine for feedback, but the "first real retrieval" should come after a delay of at least 1 day (Karpicke & Roediger 2007). Ideally, in the first months, the first *scheduled* spaced re-test comes 1–2 weeks later, then the 3–5-week gap.
- **Rule 2.3:** Equal vs expanding intervals do not matter for final retention (Latimier 2021). Choose **expanding** for error items, because it keeps them retrievable during the year (Kang 2014). Choose whatever is convenient for whole-unit reviews. A practical expanding ladder for missed items is about +1–2 days → +1 week → +3–4 weeks → +2–3 months (extrapolation).
- **Rule 2.4:** Every item or problem type needs a **last review within ~1–4 weeks of the exam**, because the optimal-gap ratio rises sharply at short RIs. That makes the last ~6–8 weeks a dense, fully cumulative review phase.
- **Rule 2.5:** Over-drilling one topic in a single session (overlearning) is wasted time for a test months away (Rohrer & Taylor 2006). Cap blocked practice of a new type at the point of first correct solutions, then move on and come back later.
- **Caveat:** Cepeda's ridgeline comes from **two-session** studies with **trivia facts**. With many sessions, each successful spaced retrieval raises the item's "stability," so later intervals can be much longer. That is what FSRS and SuperMemo model (Q3). The table gives the *first* gap; later gaps should come from an algorithm or from expanding multiples.

### Gaps
- The exact Cepeda 2008 optimal gaps per RI, the N, and the percent gain of optimal spacing over massed study were not checked, because the full text was blocked.
- There is no direct evidence on the optimal gap for **multi-step math problem types** at a 6–12-month RI. Math spacing studies use RIs of weeks (Rohrer & Taylor 2006; Rohrer 2015/2020: 1–30 days).
- There is no direct evidence on spacing for **reading-comprehension skill** (국어), as opposed to retention of content.
- There is no evidence on how spacing interacts with a ~35 h/week workload. Studies use brief exposures, not year-long intensive study.

## Q3. Spaced repetition systems: target-retention settings and the evidence behind them; Duolingo half-life regression

### Takeaway
SRS defaults cluster at **~90% target retention**: FSRS/Anki "desired retention 0.90" and SuperMemo's 10% forgetting index. The evidence for this setting is **engineering and simulation evidence** (workload vs retention curves fit to millions of reviews), **not** RCTs on exam outcomes. Workload has a minimum. Above ~0.90–0.95, reviews rise steeply for small gains, and below the optimum, forgetting and relearning increase the work.

### Cited Findings
- **FSRS4Anki wiki, "The optimal retention."** By choosing desired retention, one can minimize workload or maximize knowledge. **Workload as a function of retention has a minimum**: higher retention shortens intervals and adds reviews, while lower retention causes more forgetting and relearning. Before Anki 24.04 the optimizer maximized knowledge acquisition. **From 24.04 it minimizes workload ÷ knowledge.** Knowledge is defined as the sum of recall probabilities. Curves come from simulations with default FSRS parameters and vary between users. [D/engineering; based on large user review logs] — [FSRS wiki: The optimal retention](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention)
- **FSRS4Anki docs and forum.** **0.90 is a good starting point.** Higher values increase workload. Going below the simulator's "optimal" value is generally not advised. For exam preparation, forum advice is to start lower and raise the target or use filtered decks closer to the exam if time allows. Community guides suggest 0.75–0.90 for lighter workload and ≥0.94 only if the extra reviews are acceptable. [D] — [FSRS4Anki docs (Algolia mirror)](https://docsearch.algolia.com/mcp/docs/repo/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki); [Anki forum: FSRS retention for exam](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/need-advice-on-fsrs-retention-for-exam/39457); [AnkiHub community thread](https://community.ankihub.net/t/anki-fsrs-retention-for-im-shelf-too-much-above-90/424027)
- **SuperMemo.** The default "requested forgetting index" is 10% (≈90% retention); lower values sharply increase repetitions. ⚠ unverified: recalled from SuperMemo documentation and not fetched this session. [D] — [SuperMemo help: Forgetting index](https://help.supermemo.org/wiki/Forgetting_index)
- **Settles & Meeder (2016), ACL, "A Trainable Spaced Repetition Model for Language Learning" (Duolingo half-life regression, HLR).** HLR estimates the memory "half-life" of each word from features of practice history. It **cut recall-prediction error by 45%+** compared with baselines (Pimsleur, Leitner, logistic regression), using ~13 million learning traces, and **raised daily student engagement by 12%** in a live user study. Caveats: the 12% is engagement, not learning outcomes, and an independent re-analysis (Papoušek) found much lower discrimination (AUC ≈ 0.54) on a different split. [B for prediction; no exam-outcome evidence; adults; L2 vocabulary] — [ACL Anthology P16-1174](https://preview.aclanthology.org/landing_page/P16-1174); [Duolingo HLR repo](https://www.github.com/duolingo/halflife-regression); [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-we-learn-how-you-learn); [Papoušek re-analysis](https://papousek.github.io/analysis-of-half-life-regression-model-made-by-duolingo.html)

### Inferences
- **Rule 3.1:** Use an SRS (Anki + FSRS) for **discrete, recall-type content**: 영어 vocabulary and usage, 한국사 facts and chronology, 탐구 definitions, formulas, and key graphs, plus "concept cards" made from mistakes. Start desired retention at **0.90**. Lower it to **0.85** if daily reviews crowd out problem practice (watch the review load at 35 h/week). Raise it to **0.92–0.95** only in the final ~6–8 weeks, for high-value cards, accepting more reviews (forum guidance plus the workload curve).
- **Rule 3.2:** Run the FSRS optimizer after a few weeks or ~1,000 reviews, so intervals fit the learner's own forgetting curve (FSRS wiki: curves vary by learner).
- **Rule 3.3 (bridging to practice exams, extrapolation):** The ~90% target is consistent with the testing-effect finding that successful retrieval plus feedback is the best condition (Q1). So the *spaced-review* section of each practice exam should target ~80–90% correct. If a review section scores below ~70%, intervals were too long or the material was never learned. Shorten the next gap for those items instead of moving on.
- SRS algorithms optimize *item* recall probability. They do not handle problem-solving *types* or interleaving. Practice-exam scheduling for 수학 and 과학 calculation types needs a separate "problem-type deck" or log (Q4, Q7).

### Gaps
- I found **no RCT showing that a 0.90 target gives better exam scores** than 0.80 or 0.95. The evidence is simulation-based (workload per unit of knowledge).
- The SuperMemo default forgetting index (10%) was not verified this session.
- There is no evidence on SRS for multi-step problem schemas.

## Q4. Interleaving: effect sizes, when it helps or hurts, blocked-then-interleaved

### Takeaway
Interleaving (mixing problem types) has a **moderate overall effect (g ≈ 0.42)** that depends strongly on **similarity**. It helps most when **different categories look alike**, which forces discrimination, and when material is complex. It is near zero for expository text and **hurts for word lists (g = −0.39)**. In middle-school math, a large preregistered cluster RCT found **d = 0.83** after a 1-month delay. Learners rate interleaving as worse even when it works, and it takes more time.

### Cited Findings
- **Rohrer & Taylor (2007), *Instructional Science* 35:481–498, "The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning."** College students practiced finding the volume of solids. Mixed practice gave **~63% vs ~20% correct** on a 1-week test, even though *practice-phase* accuracy was **lower** for mixers (~60% vs ~89%). ⚠ unverified: numbers recalled. [C; lab; **adults**; math] — [DOI 10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8)
- **Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 107(3):900–908, "Interleaved practice improves mathematics learning."** A classroom study with 7th graders on graph and slope problem types. The interleaved benefit was **moderate at a 1-day delay (d ≈ 0.42)** and **large at a 30-day delay (d ≈ 0.79)**. The d values come from a garbled preview and should be checked. [B; **classroom; children (~12–13 y)**; math] — [IES award page](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/interleaved-mathematics-practice?ID=1184); [Bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/interleaved-practice-improves-mathematics-learning/814688664780013569-11152); [Univ. Iowa summary PDF](https://learning.uiowa.edu/sites/learning.uiowa.edu/files/2026-03/Mix-It-Up-The-Benefits-of-Interleaved-Practice.pdf)
- **Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 112(1):40–52, DOI 10.1037/edu0000367.** A **preregistered cluster RCT** with **54 7th-grade classes, 787 students, 5 schools, 1 district**. Classes periodically did interleaved or blocked assignments over **4 months**, then both groups did an interleaved review. On an **unannounced test 1 month later, interleaved 61% vs blocked 38%, d = 0.83, 95% CI [0.68, 0.97]**. **Every teacher reported that interleaved assignments took more time.** The study met WWC standards without reservations. [A; **classroom; children**; math] — [IES WWC review](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770); [IES efficacy award](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/awards/efficacy-study-interleaved-mathematics-practice?ID=1790); [Aminer record](https://aminer.org/pub/5d0b00728607575390fc671e/a-randomized-controlled-trial-of-interleaved-mathematics-practice)
- **Brunmair & Richter (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11):1029–1052, DOI 10.1037/bul0000209, "Similarity matters."** **59 studies, 238 effect sizes, 158 samples.** A **moderate overall interleaving effect**, cited elsewhere as **g = 0.42** (secondary). Interleaving effects were **larger when categories were more similar to each other, when items within a category were less similar, and for more complex material**. By material: **paintings g = 0.67**; **expository texts g = 0.21 (n.s.)**; **words g = −0.39 (blocking better)**; **mathematical tasks: small but significant**. [A; mixed lab + classroom; mostly adults; visual, math, verbal] — [PubMed 31556629](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/); [author manuscript (Würzburg)](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf); [x-mol abstract](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1213055298318831616). The paintings and expository-text subgroup values come from a search snippet attributing them to "a 2024 paper"; the citing paper was not identified with certainty (possibly [this PDF](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PRKL_2024.pdf)) [D]
- **Mechanism / confound.** In Rohrer's math designs, interleaving also **spaces** practice of each type and requires choosing a strategy, which blocked sets give away. The benefit is attributed to *discrimination* (deciding which method fits) plus spacing. [B] — [Rohrer 2020 summary (IES)](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770); [Brunmair & Richter abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)
- **Metacognitive illusion.** Learners judged massed (blocked) study as better even when interleaving produced better category induction. ⚠ unverified: recalled from Kornell & Bjork (2008), *Psychological Science* 19:585–592, "Learning concepts and categories: Is spacing the 'enemy of induction'?" [B; lab; adults; paintings] — [DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02127.x)
- **Blocked vs interleaved by discriminability.** Blocking helped more for categories that are **highly discriminable** (low between-category similarity), and interleaving helped for **highly similar** categories. ⚠ unverified: recalled from Carvalho & Goldstone (2014), *Memory & Cognition* 42:481–495, "Putting category learning in order." This is consistent with Brunmair & Richter's similarity moderator. [C; lab; adults; visual categories] — [DOI 10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-013-0371-0)

### Inferences
- **Rule 4.1 (blocked → interleaved; moderate evidence + extrapolation):** Introduce each **new** problem type in a short blocked set: worked example(s), then a few same-type problems until the first correct solutions (Q1 Rule 1.4). From then on, that type appears **only in interleaved sets** mixed with earlier types. This matches the Rohrer classroom designs, where blocked lesson instruction came first and interleaved assignments followed, and Brunmair & Richter's finding that interleaving helps once categories are known and confusable. Interleaving *before* any acquisition is not supported for novices.
- **Rule 4.2 (strong evidence):** **Interleave confusable types**: problems that look alike but need different methods, e.g., 수학 수열 vs 급수 settings, 지수·로그 vs 함수 그래프 reading, 미적분 극값 vs 넓이 problems; 과학탐구 calculation types; 영어 빈칸 vs 순서·삽입 items. Mixing types that are **dissimilar and already easy to tell apart** adds little discrimination benefit, though it still gives spacing.
- **Rule 4.3 (evidence: words g = −0.39):** Do **not** interleave pure vocabulary or list memorization to force mixing. Leave that content to the SRS (Q3).
- **Rule 4.4:** Budget **~20–30% more time per interleaved set** than per blocked set. Rohrer 2020 teachers reported longer completion times; the 20–30% figure is an extrapolation, not a measured value. Also expect **lower scores on interleaved practice sets** than on blocked ones (Rohrer & Taylor 2007). That is the point, not a sign of failure.
- **Adult vs child:** The large classroom math RCTs are with 7th graders. The adult math evidence is a lab study (Rohrer & Taylor 2007). Generalizing to an adult 수능 learner is plausible, but the effect-size transfer is unknown.

### Gaps
- The exact Brunmair & Richter values for overall g (0.42 is a secondary citation), the math subgroup g, and age or prior-knowledge moderators were not verified, because the full text was blocked.
- Rohrer 2015 exact d values and sample size were not verified (the preview was garbled).
- **The exact proportion of current-lesson vs earlier-lesson problems in Rohrer's interleaved assignments was not found.** This would directly inform Q7.
- I found no study of interleaving across **whole subjects** (e.g., 국어 then 수학 on the same day) as distinct from within-subject problem types.

## Q5. Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013): utility ratings of the 10 techniques

### Takeaway
Only **practice testing** and **distributed practice** were rated **high utility**. **Interleaved practice**, **elaborative interrogation**, and **self-explanation** were moderate. **Summarization, highlighting/underlining, the keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, and rereading** were rated low.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1):4–58, "Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology." **High utility: practice testing (self-testing or practice tests on to-be-learned material) and distributed practice (spreading study over time).** **Low utility: summarization, rereading, highlighting/underlining, the keyword mnemonic, and imagery use for text learning.** Rereading and highlighting are the most common student strategies but do not consistently boost performance. Ratings reflect both efficacy and generalizability across learners, materials, criterion tasks, and settings. [A (expert review of hundreds of studies)] — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm); [Kent State release](https://www.kent.edu/node/230016); paper DOI [10.1177/1529100612453266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)
- **Moderate utility: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice.** These are the three remaining of the ten techniques after the two high-rated and five low-rated ones; ⚠ not seen verbatim this session. Interleaving was rated moderate mainly because evidence was then limited to a narrow range of materials (math, motor tasks, category learning). That was before the Rohrer 2015/2020 classroom RCTs and the Brunmair & Richter 2019 meta-analysis. — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html); DOI [10.1177/1529100612453266](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)

### Inferences
- The two high-utility techniques are exactly the two levers of a practice-exam *schedule*: what to test (testing) and when to re-test (spacing). Interleaving is how to compose each exam, and its rating would probably be revised upward for math given the 2020 RCT (d = 0.83). That revision is my inference, not the authors' statement.
- **Rule 5.1:** Replace rereading and highlighting of notes and 개념서 with closed-book retrieval: blank-page recall of a chapter, then checking. Use self-explanation for worked solutions after a miss (moderate utility), especially in 수학 and 과학.

### Gaps
- The per-technique evidence breakdowns (age, material, delay) in the monograph were not accessed. Only press-level summaries could be reached.

## Q6. Cumulative exams and mixed review: evidence for including old material in every test

### Takeaway
Classroom evidence supports making tests cumulative. Including even **~20% old content** in each unit exam improved long-term retention for **lower-scoring** students (Lawrence 2013). Cumulative finals improved retention up to **18 months** later, more for introductory than advanced students (Khanna et al. 2013). Expecting a cumulative test boosts the benefit of interim tests (Szpunar 2007). The evidence is from college psychology courses and is quasi-experimental.

### Cited Findings
- **Lawrence (2013), *Teaching of Psychology* 40(1):15–19, "Cumulative exams in the introductory psychology course."** In the cumulative section, the 2nd and 3rd exams each included **10 questions on material from previous exams**, described as **~20% cumulative content**. **High-scoring students' long-term retention was unaffected, but low-scoring students remembered more** with cumulative exams. [B-/C; classroom quasi-experiment; **adults (college)**; psychology content] — [Faculty Focus summary](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/educational-assessment/examining-the-benefits-of-cumulative-tests-and-finals/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0098628313487458)
- **Khanna, Badura Brack & Finken (2013), *Teaching of Psychology* 40(3):175–182, "Short- and long-term effects of cumulative finals on student learning."** Students in courses with **cumulative finals retained more** than those with non-cumulative finals on retention tests **up to 18 months** after the course. **Introductory students benefited more** than upper-division students. [B-/C; classroom, quasi-experimental; adults; psychology] — [MRU ISoTL summary](https://blogs.mtroyal.ca/isotl/2013/08/06/short-and-long-term-effects-of-cumulative-finals-on-student-learning/); [Creighton record](https://researchworks.creighton.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Short--and-Long-Term-Effects-of-Cumulative/991005931127602656)
- **Szpunar, McDermott & Roediger (2007).** Expecting a final cumulative test strengthened the benefit of initial testing. [C; lab; adults; verbal] — [PubMed 17910184](https://www.pubmed.gov/17910184/)
- **Yang et al. (2021).** In the classroom quizzing meta-analysis, **number of test repetitions** and **treatment duration** were significant moderators, which supports repeated cumulative quizzing over a term. [A; classroom] — [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- **Rohrer et al. (2020).** Interleaved assignments are a form of cumulative mixed review, since each problem set draws on earlier lessons. They gave d = 0.83 at a 1-month delay. [A; classroom; children; math] — [IES WWC](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)

### Inferences
- **Rule 6.1:** Every practice exam should be **at least partly cumulative**. ~20% old content is the lowest level with direct classroom support (Lawrence 2013). Higher fractions are extrapolations supported by spacing and interleaving theory (Q2, Q4).
- **Rule 6.2:** The benefit was concentrated in **lower-scoring** and **introductory** students (Lawrence; Khanna). That suggests more cumulative review for the learner's **weakest subjects** and **early in the year**, and less for subjects already at a ceiling. This is an extrapolation.
- 수능 itself is a fully cumulative, fully interleaved exam. So the late phase of the practice-exam sequence should converge to **100% cumulative, CSAT-format mocks**, which also satisfies transfer-appropriate processing and format matching (Yang 2021 moderators; Pan & Rickard 2018 response congruency).

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes, sample sizes, and designs for Lawrence (2013) and Khanna et al. (2013) were not obtained; only summaries were reachable. Both are **non-randomized** course comparisons.
- I found no study that **varies the proportion** of cumulative content (e.g., 20% vs 40% vs 60%) to find an optimum.

## Q7. Concrete composition: what fraction of each practice exam should be new vs spaced review vs interleaved mixed review?

### Takeaway
**No study gives an optimal new/old ratio for practice exams.** The direct anchors are: ≥20% cumulative content helps (Lawrence 2013); fully interleaved cumulative review works in math (Rohrer 2020); spaced beats massed retrieval (g = 0.74); and first-review gaps should be ~5–10% of the RI when the exam is a year away. The schedule below is **an EXTRAPOLATION** that combines these. It shifts from mostly new material toward fully cumulative CSAT-format mocks.

### Cited Findings (anchors for the extrapolation)
- ~20% old content in each exam improved long-term retention for lower scorers — [Lawrence 2013 via Faculty Focus](https://www.facultyfocus.com/articles/educational-assessment/examining-the-benefits-of-cumulative-tests-and-finals/)
- Cumulative finals improved retention up to 18 months later — [Khanna et al. 2013 (MRU ISoTL)](https://blogs.mtroyal.ca/isotl/2013/08/06/short-and-long-term-effects-of-cumulative-finals-on-student-learning/)
- Spaced retrieval beats massed retrieval (g = 0.74), and expanding ≈ uniform (g = 0.034) — [Latimier et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)
- Optimal gap ≈ 20–40% of the RI at 1 week, ≈ 5–10% at 1 year — [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- Interleaved assignments in math: d = 0.83 after 1 month, but they take more time — [Rohrer et al. 2020 (WWC)](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Study/88770)
- Interleaving helps for similar or confusable categories and hurts for word lists — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31556629/)
- Classroom quizzing benefits increase with more repetitions and feedback — [Yang et al. 2021](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Transfer is stronger when practice and final test responses match and initial performance is high — [Pan & Rickard 2018 (author MS)](https://sc-pan.github.io/pdf/PR_2018W.pdf)
- SRS default target retention ≈ 0.90; workload has a minimum — [FSRS wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention)

### Inferences (ALL EXTRAPOLATION — not directly tested)
**Proposed composition of the weekly practice exam by phase** (per subject, by item count):

| Phase (approx. dates) | New (this week's content; may be blocked within its section) | Spaced review (content from ≥ the gap in Q2; scheduled, mostly blocked by unit) | Interleaved mixed review (confusable types from all covered units, shuffled) | Full CSAT-format mocks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Acquisition** (months 1–4: Nov 2026–Feb 2027) | **~50–60%** | **~25–30%** (units 3–5+ weeks old) | **~15–20%** | Diagnostic full mock about every 4–6 weeks |
| **B. Consolidation** (months 5–8: Mar–Jun 2027) | **~30–40%** | **~30–35%** | **~30–35%** | 1 full mock every ~2 weeks (e.g., use the June 모의평가 as a checkpoint) |
| **C. Integration** (months 9–10: Jul–Sep 2027) | **~10–20%** (gap-filling) | **~20–30%** (error-item ladder) | **~50–60%** | 1 full mock / week (incl. the September 모의평가) |
| **D. Final** (last ~6–8 weeks: Oct–Nov 2027) | **~0–5%** | covered inside the mocks + error re-tests | **≈100% cumulative** | 2–3 full mocks / week under timed conditions; each followed by a full error review |

- **Why these numbers.** The floor of ~20% old content comes from Lawrence (2013). The old-content share then rises each phase because (a) the amount of old material grows, (b) for later-learned material the ideal gaps shorten as the exam nears (Cepeda 2008), and (c) 수능 is fully interleaved, so later practice must match the target format (transfer-appropriate processing and format matching: Yang 2021; Pan & Rickard 2018). The specific percentages are judgment calls, not measured optima.
- **Rule 7.1, re-test cadence for missed items** (expanding is fine per Kang 2014; equal intervals would also do per Latimier 2021): **+1–2 days** (re-solve after studying the solution) → **+1 week** → **+3–4 weeks** → **+2–3 months**, then into the general pool. Each step needs a *cold* solve without the solution, and the item drops out after 2–3 consecutive spaced successes (successive-relearning logic, Rawson et al. 2013; ⚠ unverified).
- **Rule 7.2, whole-unit review cadence:** First spaced re-test of a unit at **3–5 weeks** if learned in months 1–6, **2–3 weeks** in months 7–9, and **~1 week** in months 10–11 (Q2 ladder). After that, each unit reappears roughly every **1–2 months** until Phase D, when everything is reviewed at least once in the last ~4 weeks.
- **Rule 7.3, interleaving content:** The interleaved section should be **built around confusable pairs** (Brunmair & Richter). It should include only types already acquired in a blocked set (Rule 4.1), and new types enter it the week after they are introduced. The SRS handles vocabulary and lists; they should not be interleaved (words g = −0.39).
- **Rule 7.4, difficulty targets:** Aim for spaced-review sections at **~75–90% correct**. If below ~70%, shorten that unit's next gap and add feedback study. If above ~95% for two cycles, lengthen the gap. Interleaved sections will run lower; ~60–80% is acceptable and expected (Rohrer & Taylor 2007 practice-phase deficit). These bands are extrapolations from the retrieval-success and feedback moderators (Rowland 2014; Pan & Rickard 2018) and the FSRS 0.9 convention.
- **Rule 7.5, feedback protocol:** Every exam is followed the same day by answer checking and solution study. Delayed or next-day feedback review is also acceptable; feedback is the key moderator. Every miss and every guess goes into the error ladder (Rule 7.1).
- **Rule 7.6, time budget at ~35 h/week (extrapolation):** Practice exams plus their feedback review should take roughly **30–40% of weekly hours in Phase A**, rising to **~60–70% in Phase D**. The remainder goes to new learning (worked examples, concept study) and SRS reviews. SRS review load should be watched: lower the FSRS target to 0.85 if reviews exceed ~45–60 min/day (Q3). These are scheduling judgments with no direct evidence.
- **Rule 7.7, subject-specific weighting (extrapolation):** Following Lawrence and Khanna (larger benefit for weaker or introductory students), **give weaker subjects a larger review fraction** (e.g., +10 percentage points) and give subjects near ceiling more full mocks instead.

### Gaps
- **No empirical optimum exists** for the new / spaced / interleaved split; every percentage above is extrapolation.
- No study tests practice-exam scheduling over a **12-month horizon at ~35 h/week**, for **adults returning to high-school content**, or for **Korean CSAT subjects** specifically.
- I did not find the proportion of previously learned problems in Rohrer's interleaved assignments, which would best anchor the math split.
- No evidence was found on the optimal **frequency of full-length mocks** (e.g., 1 vs 3 per week) versus targeted section tests. Fatigue and the cost of review time are untested.
- Testing-effect evidence for **국어 reading comprehension** (a skill, not retrieval of facts) is lacking. Its share of review may be better set by skill-practice volume than by spacing ratios.
